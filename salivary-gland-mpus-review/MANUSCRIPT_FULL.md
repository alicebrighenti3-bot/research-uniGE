# Multiparametric Ultrasound of the Salivary Glands: From Normal Sonoanatomy to Integrated Diagnostic Pathways for Inflammatory, Autoimmune, and Neoplastic Disease

**Running title:** Multiparametric US of the Salivary Glands

## Abstract (structured, ~300 words — draft)

**Background.** Ultrasound is the first-line imaging modality for the superficial,
high-frequency-accessible major salivary glands, but conventional grayscale and Doppler
assessment has recognized limits in tissue characterization. Multiparametric ultrasound
(mpUS) — the combined, structured use of B-mode, color/power Doppler, microvascular
imaging, elastography, and contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) — has been proposed to
close this gap.

**Purpose.** To critically review normal salivary gland sonoanatomy and the evidence
base for mpUS across inflammatory, obstructive, autoimmune, benign neoplastic, and
malignant neoplastic disease, and to define which components of an integrated mpUS
approach are validated for current practice versus promising or experimental.

**Methods.** Narrative review with structured evidence appraisal, prioritizing
systematic reviews, meta-analyses, consensus/guideline statements, and comparative
diagnostic-accuracy studies identified through literature search current to 2026.
Evidence is explicitly graded as consolidated, promising-but-unvalidated, or
experimental/emerging, and discordant findings between studies are analyzed for their
likely technical or population-based origin rather than resolved by selective citation.

**Results.** B-mode plus Doppler ultrasound remains validated for first-line triage and
for sialolithiasis, where diagnostic accuracy is high and consistently reproduced.
Grey-scale ultrasound scoring (OMERACT system) is the most rigorously validated advanced
application, for Sjögren's syndrome, under trained/structured reading conditions.
Elastography and microvascular imaging add diagnostically relevant information for
benign tumor subtyping but do not support a universal, vendor-independent numeric
cut-off; meta-analyses addressing the same clinical question report discordant pooled
accuracy, attributable chiefly to technique and vendor heterogeneity. CEUS is well
supported for differentiating pleomorphic adenoma from Warthin tumor but lacks a pooled
accuracy estimate for malignancy detection. Core needle biopsy shows consistently higher
sensitivity than fine-needle aspiration for malignancy across independent comparative
studies. Genuinely algorithmic multiparametric data fusion, US–MRI fusion, and most
thermal ablation techniques beyond radiofrequency ablation remain experimental or
unstudied for this organ.

**Conclusion.** mpUS should be integrated into salivary gland diagnostic pathways as a
structured, technique-by-technique reasoning process rather than a single validated
test; this review proposes such a workflow while explicitly flagging its unvalidated,
expert-opinion status.

**Keywords:** salivary glands; ultrasonography; elastography; contrast-enhanced
ultrasound; microvascular imaging; Sjögren syndrome; fine-needle aspiration; core needle
biopsy

## 1. Introduction

Ultrasound is the first-line imaging modality for the major salivary glands. The
parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands are superficial structures accessible to
high-frequency linear-array transducers, which afford spatial resolution that neither
computed tomography (CT) nor magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can match for the
glandular parenchyma itself and for the immediately adjacent duct system. Ultrasound
adds real-time assessment of vascularity, dynamic evaluation with compression and
sialagogue provocation, and the possibility of image-guided sampling in the same
sitting as diagnostic imaging — capabilities that cross-sectional imaging does not
offer at the point of care. Its limitations are equally well established: a narrow
field of view that cannot reliably assess the deep parotid lobe, the parapharyngeal
space, or perineural extension along the facial or lingual nerves; marked operator
dependence; and no direct role in nodal or distant staging of malignant disease, where
CT and MRI (and, for staging, PET/CT) remain indispensable (Bialek et al., *RadioGraphics*
2006; Gritzmann et al., *Eur Radiol* 2003).

Over the past two decades, ultrasound practice in this organ system has moved from a
purely grayscale, morphology-based examination toward what is increasingly described as
*multiparametric ultrasound* (mpUS): the structured, combined use of B-mode imaging,
color and power Doppler, microvascular imaging techniques capable of depicting slow
flow (e.g., Superb Microvascular Imaging, SMI), strain and shear-wave elastography, and
contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), interpreted together rather than in isolation
(Martino et al., *Gland Surg* 2020). For the purposes of this review, **mpUS is defined
as the deliberate, protocol-driven acquisition and joint interpretation of two or more
of these ultrasound domains — morphologic, vascular, mechanical (stiffness), and
perfusion-based — applied to the same lesion or gland**, as distinct from the
incidental or non-systematic use of a single ancillary technique.

This evolution matters clinically because the differential diagnosis of salivary gland
disease spans mechanistically distinct categories — obstructive/inflammatory (sialolithiasis,
sialadenitis), autoimmune/systemic (Sjögren's syndrome, IgG4-related disease), benign
neoplastic, and malignant neoplastic — that can present with overlapping clinical
findings (glandular swelling, pain, a palpable mass) but require different management
pathways. Grayscale ultrasound alone has documented, non-trivial error rates in this
discrimination, particularly for malignancy, where sensitivity is frequently lower than
specificity even in well-conducted single-center series (Rzepakowska et al., *Eur Arch
Otorhinolaryngol* 2017). The rationale for mpUS is that vascular, mechanical, and
perfusion information, layered onto morphology, can narrow this diagnostic uncertainty
and better select which lesions require tissue sampling, which can be safely observed,
and which require cross-sectional imaging before any decision is made.

Whether this rationale is fully realized in the current evidence base is a separate
question from whether it is plausible, and is the central question this review is
organized around: **how can conventional and advanced multiparametric ultrasound be
integrated to improve the diagnosis, characterization, tissue sampling, and management
of normal and pathological salivary glands, and which components of this approach are
sufficiently validated for current clinical practice?** As will be shown throughout this
review, the honest answer varies by technique and by clinical question: some
applications (sialolithiasis diagnosis, OMERACT grey-scale scoring in Sjögren's
syndrome, the superiority of core needle biopsy over fine-needle aspiration for
malignancy detection) rest on consistent, multiply-reproduced evidence; others
(elastographic and Doppler cut-offs for benign-versus-malignant differentiation,
algorithmic multiparametric data fusion) remain promising but unvalidated, or frankly
experimental. This review does not attempt to catalogue every published sonographic
sign of every salivary gland disease; rather, it aims to show, technique by technique
and disease by disease, where mpUS measurably changes clinical reasoning and where it
does not yet.

Several existing publications cover overlapping ground and are explicitly acknowledged
here rather than left for the reader to discover independently. Martino et al.
(*Gland Surgery*, 2020) published a narrative review under an almost identical
conceptual framing, restricted to the parotid gland and predating essentially all of
the microvascular-imaging, most of the CEUS-quantification, and all of the
artificial-intelligence literature discussed below. A 2025 continuing-medical-education
review by Johnson et al. (*Ultraschall in der Medizin*) covers sonography of salivary
gland tumors and disorders in the same target-journal category as the present work; its
precise technique structure was not independently verifiable during preparation of this
manuscript and should be considered a closely related, contemporaneous resource rather
than a document this review claims to supersede. The present review differs from both
in scope (all major glands and the full disease spectrum, including lymph nodes, in a
single technique-by-technique structure), in its explicit grading of evidence strength
and analysis of discordance between studies rather than descriptive cataloguing, in its
integration of interventional ultrasound into a single diagnostic pathway, and in its
currency through 2026.
## 2. Normal anatomy and ultrasound technique

### 2.1 Parotid gland

The parotid is the largest major salivary gland, situated in the preauricular and
retromandibular region, bounded superficially by skin and subcutaneous fat, deeply by
the styloid process and its associated musculature, anteriorly by the masseter muscle,
and posteriorly by the external ear and sternocleidomastoid muscle. On ultrasound it
appears as a homogeneously hyperechoic gland relative to adjacent muscle, owing to its
fat content, with a fine, uniform echotexture. Ultrasound cannot directly visualize the
facial nerve, which is not an ultrasound landmark itself but is localized indirectly:
the retromandibular vein, which runs superficial and lateral to the nerve trunk within
the gland, is the standard surrogate landmark separating the notional superficial and
deep lobes for reporting purposes (Bialek et al., *RadioGraphics* 2006). An
ultrasonographic technique using Stensen's duct as an alternative localizing criterion
for the facial nerve plane relative to intraparotid tumors has also been described,
though it is less widely used than the retromandibular-vein landmark. The deep parotid
lobe and any parapharyngeal extension of disease are not reliably assessed by
ultrasound because of acoustic shadowing from the mandibular ramus; this is one of the
central, unavoidable limitations of the technique and a principal reason MRI remains
necessary for deep-lobe or parapharyngeal disease.

Stensen's duct (the parotid duct) courses anteriorly over the masseter, turns medially
at the anterior masseteric border, and pierces the buccinator to open opposite the
second upper molar. On normal ultrasound the duct is **not visualized unless dilated**;
non-visualization is itself the expected normal finding, not an indication of technical
failure. Transient ductal distension can be provoked with a sialagogue (e.g., ascorbic
acid or lemon juice) to aid visualization when duct patency or a subtle stricture is in
question, though this maneuver is more a diagnostic adjunct for suspected obstruction
than part of the routine baseline examination.

An accessory parotid gland — a discrete lobule of parotid tissue anterior to the main
gland, superficial to the masseter and related to the buccal branches of the facial
nerve and the transverse facial artery — is a recognized and clinically relevant
anatomical variant, because tumors can arise within it and be overlooked if it is not
specifically sought with a high-frequency transducer. Reported prevalence figures for
the accessory parotid gland vary substantially between series (from roughly one-fifth to
over two-thirds of subjects, depending on whether cadaveric, CT, or clinical-imaging
methodology is used), reflecting differences in detection method rather than true
biological variability, and no single figure should be presented as definitive.

### 2.2 Submandibular gland

The submandibular gland lies in the submandibular triangle, bounded by the anterior and
posterior bellies of the digastric muscle and the inferior border of the mandible, and
wraps around the posterior free edge of the mylohyoid muscle to give rise to a smaller
deep extension. It is typically slightly less echogenic and coarser in texture than the
parotid gland but remains hyperechoic relative to adjacent muscle in health. The facial
artery and vein course along its posterior and lateral surface at the mandibular angle
and are consistent vascular landmarks on grayscale and color Doppler imaging.

Wharton's duct (the submandibular duct) runs anteromedially along the floor of the
mouth, deep to the mylohyoid and superficial to the hyoglossus and genioglossus
muscles, to open at the sublingual caruncle. As with Stensen's duct, the normal duct is
not visualized on ultrasound unless dilated. A formal, society-endorsed proposal for
standardized submandibular gland ultrasound terminology and technique was published by
the Salivary Gland Committee of the American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck
Surgery (Hoffman et al., *Laryngoscope Investig Otolaryngol* 2024); at the time of this
review's preparation, a corresponding standardization document for the parotid gland
had not been identified as published, and none for the sublingual or minor glands — an
explicit gap that should be closed by future methodological work.

### 2.3 Sublingual gland and minor salivary glands

The sublingual gland, the smallest of the three major paired glands, lies in the floor
of the mouth between the mylohyoid muscle inferolaterally and the genioglossus muscle
medially, superior to the mylohyoid sling. It drains via numerous small excretory ducts
(commonly cited as roughly 8–20) that open independently along the sublingual fold or,
variably, join Wharton's duct. Its small size and deep, midline floor-of-mouth location
make complete sonographic depiction inherently more operator- and patient-dependent
than for the parotid or submandibular glands.

Minor salivary glands, distributed throughout the oral mucosa, lips, and palate, are
only inconsistently accessible to transcutaneous ultrasound; when assessed
sonographically, this is most often in the specific research/clinical context of
labial minor gland evaluation as an alternative or complement to biopsy in suspected
Sjögren's syndrome, where a minimum number of visualized glands and a minimum
cross-sectional area threshold have been used as feasibility criteria in at least one
comparative study against histopathology. This should be regarded as a specialized,
disease-context application rather than part of the routine normal-anatomy survey.

### 2.4 Lymph nodes and vascular anatomy

Intraparotid and periparotid lymph nodes are a normal anatomical finding, most
consistently encountered near the parotid tail and along the course of the
retromandibular vein; small, oval nodes with a preserved echogenic fatty hilum are
expected and should not be over-interpreted. Periglandular cervical nodal groups
(submandibular, upper jugular chain) are routinely included in a complete examination
given their relevance to both inflammatory and malignant disease of the glands they
drain. Relevant regional vascular anatomy includes the external carotid artery and its
branches (facial, superficial temporal, transverse facial, maxillary arteries) and the
retromandibular and facial veins, all of which serve both as anatomical landmarks and as
structures that must be identified and avoided during image-guided intervention (see
Section 14).

### 2.5 Examination technique

A high-frequency linear-array transducer is used for all three major glands; reported
frequency ranges vary slightly across sources (commonly cited ranges span approximately
5–14 MHz), without full consensus on a single standard, and this variability should
itself be acknowledged rather than presented as settled. A lower-frequency curved-array
transducer (approximately 5–7 MHz) is a useful adjunct for reaching the deep parotid
lobe in selected patients, accepting the trade-off in resolution. The patient is
typically examined supine with the neck extended and rotated away from the side under
examination; both transverse and longitudinal scan planes are obtained for each gland.
Bilateral, side-to-side comparison is explicitly recommended in recent guidance, with
the examination conventionally beginning on the presumed normal side (Johnson et al.,
*Ultraschall Med* 2025). Gain, depth, and focal-zone position should be optimized for
the superficial location of these glands, and tissue harmonic imaging can be used to
improve contrast resolution and reduce artifact, particularly in patients with a larger
body habitus, though dedicated salivary-gland-specific comparative data on harmonic
versus fundamental imaging were not identified in this review's literature search.
Probe-compression technique, while a general principle of superficial-structure
ultrasound (useful, for instance, in distinguishing a compressible vascular structure
or in displacing overlying bowel gas elsewhere in the body), has not been specifically
studied for the salivary glands in the literature identified here and is not addressed
by dedicated protocols.

### 2.6 Normal size, echogenicity, and the absence of universal reference ranges

Normal gland dimensions have been reported since at least the late 1990s from
small, single-center biometric studies, but **no universally accepted, cross-vendor
normal size range for the parotid or submandibular gland exists**, a limitation stated
explicitly by the most recent standardization effort in this field (Hoffman et al.,
*Laryngoscope Investig Otolaryngol* 2024). Echogenicity is similarly not a fixed,
binary normal/abnormal parameter: in the largest modern cohort identified addressing
this question (n = 969 adults with sonographically normal glands), the submandibular
gland was homogeneously hypoechoic relative to the parotid in roughly a quarter of
subjects overall, and the proportion of subjects showing this relative hypoechogenicity
fell substantially with increasing age — from roughly three-fifths of the youngest age
group to just over one-fifth of those aged 50 and older (Choi et al., *Ultrasonography*
2021). Age is therefore a documented, quantifiable confound on what counts as "normal"
echogenicity, and body mass index and metabolic status (diabetes, obesity, sialosis)
have also been reported to influence glandular echogenicity, homogeneity, and border
definition, independent of any change in tissue stiffness on elastography. Practically,
this means normal-gland assessment should rely on side-to-side symmetry and on
qualitative pattern recognition calibrated to the patient's age and body habitus, rather
than on a fixed numeric size or echogenicity threshold.

Duct calibre is a further area where available reference data are frequently
misapplied: normative diameter figures for Stensen's and Wharton's ducts in the
literature derive from sialography (contrast fluoroscopy) or cadaveric morphometry, not
from ultrasound itself, and no ultrasound-specific normative duct-diameter dataset was
identified. Because the normal duct is not visualized on ultrasound, the clinically
meaningful "normal" finding is non-visualization rather than a measured calibre — a
distinction that should be made explicit whenever duct size is discussed in a
sonographic (as opposed to sialographic) context.
## 3. Conventional B-mode ultrasound

Systematic grayscale assessment of a salivary gland or focal lesion should address, in
order: overall gland size and symmetry; parenchymal echogenicity and homogeneity;
presence, number, location, and characteristics of any focal lesion (size, shape,
margin definition, internal echogenicity, presence of cystic change or necrosis,
calcification, posterior acoustic features); ductal calibre; and the relationship of
any abnormality to adjacent muscle, bone, and vessels.

**Diffuse parenchymal change.** Diffuse glandular enlargement with heterogeneous,
coarsened, or hypoechoic parenchyma is a nonspecific pattern shared by acute and chronic
sialadenitis, Sjögren's syndrome, IgG4-related disease, sarcoidosis, and, less commonly,
diffuse lymphomatous or metastatic infiltration; grayscale appearance alone cannot
reliably separate these entities, and the pattern should be considered a trigger for
further clinical, serological, and — where advanced techniques or biopsy are indicated —
multiparametric work-up rather than a diagnosis in itself (see Sections 9–10).

**Focal lesions.** Margin definition, shape, and internal echo pattern are the classical
descriptors used to stratify benign-versus-malignant risk. Well-defined margins,
smoothly lobulated shape, and homogeneous hypoechoic echotexture with posterior acoustic
enhancement are the textbook description of benign lesions such as pleomorphic adenoma,
while irregular or ill-defined margins, heterogeneous echotexture, and infiltrative
growth into adjacent tissue are the textbook description of malignancy. In practice, the
discriminating value of these signs is materially weaker than this dichotomy suggests:
in one histopathologically confirmed series, grayscale-and-Doppler assessment achieved
high specificity but only moderate sensitivity for malignancy (60% sensitivity, 95.2%
specificity, 90.3% accuracy), meaning a substantial minority of malignant tumors were
missed despite careful conventional assessment (Rzepakowska et al., *Eur Arch
Otorhinolaryngol* 2017). Margin definition in particular should not be treated as a
reliably sensitive sign: in at least one reported dataset, well-defined margins were
present in a majority of malignant tumors, not only in benign ones, and irregular shape
was similarly common in both groups — an overlap that must be stated plainly rather than
smoothed into a simple rule. A pooled meta-analysis of grayscale ultrasound (independent
of Doppler) for salivary tumor diagnosis reported similarly Doppler-independent overall
figures around 63% sensitivity and 92% specificity, again illustrating a specificity-
over-sensitivity pattern (Liu et al., *Oral Surg Oral Med Oral Pathol Oral Radiol* 2015).
This pattern — grayscale ultrasound as a better "rule-in" than "rule-out" test for
malignancy — recurs throughout the more advanced techniques discussed later in this
review and is one of its recurring themes.

**Cystic and necrotic change.** Anechoic or predominantly cystic components with thin,
regular walls are common in Warthin tumor (reported in a substantial minority to near-
half of cases in comparative series) and comparatively uncommon in pleomorphic adenoma;
irregular, thick-walled cystic or necrotic change within an otherwise solid mass raises
concern for malignancy, particularly in a rapidly enlarging lesion, but is not by itself
diagnostic of any single entity.

**Calcification and pseudocapsule.** Coarse or punctate calcification within a focal
lesion is an infrequent but recognized feature that should prompt closer scrutiny,
though it is not specific to malignancy. Pleomorphic adenoma is often described as
having a thin, well-defined "pseudocapsule" (a compressed rim of surrounding tissue
rather than a true fibrous capsule), which correlates with its typically well-defined
sonographic margin — a useful concept for understanding why margin sharpness alone is an
imperfect malignancy discriminator, since a benign tumor with true capsular invasion
risk (as in malignant transformation, discussed in Section 12) can still appear
deceptively well-marginated.

**Ductal dilation and calculi.** Duct dilation proximal to an obstructing calculus is a
reliable, well-evidenced grayscale finding, and ultrasound detection of sialoliths
themselves is one of the best-validated diagnostic applications of salivary gland
ultrasound overall (see Section 9.2), typically appearing as an echogenic focus with
posterior acoustic shadowing, though small or intraductal stones without significant
acoustic impedance mismatch can be missed, particularly distally within Wharton's duct
where mandibular shadowing can obscure the field.

**General principle.** No single grayscale feature — margin, shape, echogenicity,
cystic change, or calcification — has been shown to carry the level of specificity
needed to make an unsupported diagnosis of malignancy or of a specific histologic
subtype. Grayscale findings are best used to generate a structured differential
diagnosis and to determine whether the lesion warrants advanced multiparametric
assessment, tissue sampling, or cross-sectional imaging, rather than as a stand-alone
diagnostic endpoint (see Section 15 for pattern-based differential-diagnostic
reasoning).
## 4. Color Doppler and power Doppler

### 4.1 Technique and optimization

Color and power Doppler assessment of salivary gland lesions requires attention to
pulse repetition frequency (PRF) and wall filter settings low enough to detect the
relatively slow, low-volume flow typical of small intraglandular vessels, without
introducing excessive motion/flash artifact from patient or probe movement; gain should
be set just below the level of background noise. Box size should be limited to the
region of interest to preserve frame rate and color sensitivity, and probe pressure
should be minimized, since even moderate compression can occlude low-pressure venous
and small arterial flow and produce a falsely avascular appearance. No dedicated,
salivary-gland-specific technical-optimization literature addressing these parameters
was identified in this review's search; the recommendations above extrapolate from
general superficial-organ Doppler principles rather than from a salivary-gland-specific
validation study, and this should be understood as a technique gap, not a settled
protocol.

### 4.2 Normal and inflammatory vascular patterns

The normal parenchyma of the major salivary glands shows sparse, symmetric flow on
color/power Doppler. Acute sialadenitis characteristically produces diffuse, increased
parenchymal flow (hyperemia), a pattern that is sensitive for active inflammation but
not specific to its cause (bacterial, viral, or autoimmune). Chronic sialadenitis and
glandular atrophy tend to show reduced or absent flow. This qualitative, largely
pattern-recognition use of Doppler — present versus absent, increased versus reduced,
symmetric versus asymmetric — is well established descriptively but has not, in the
literature identified here, been reduced to a validated quantitative scoring system
outside the Sjögren's-syndrome-specific OMERACT framework discussed in Section 10.

### 4.3 Doppler in focal lesion characterization: what the evidence actually supports

Spectral Doppler parameters — resistive index (RI), pulsatility index (PI), and peak
systolic velocity (PSV) — have been proposed as quantitative discriminators between
benign and malignant salivary gland tumors since at least the classic single-center
study by Bradley et al. (*Clin Radiol* 2000), which, in a cohort of 56 patients, reported
that RI and PI values above certain thresholds were associated with malignancy with
reasonable reported sensitivity and high specificity. This remains the most frequently
cited spectral-Doppler cut-off study in this field, but it is a single, relatively
small, single-center series, and **no completed systematic review or meta-analysis
pooling RI/PI/PSV cut-offs for salivary gland tumor characterization was identified** —
a 2021-era protocol registered for exactly this purpose does not appear, from this
review's search, to have been published as a completed review. Other, smaller series
report discordant, sometimes contradictory conclusions: one study reported no significant
difference in vascularity grade between a majority of benign and a majority of malignant
tumors, with most malignant cases in that series presenting at an early, poorly
vascularized stage — a finding directly at odds with a simple "malignant = hypervascular"
heuristic. Combined color-Doppler-plus-MRI approaches have shown incremental value over
either modality alone for lesion localization and characterization in some series, but
detailed combined-accuracy figures were not consistently retrievable for this review.

**The practical implication is that spectral Doppler indices should not be presented, on
current evidence, as a validated numeric cut-off for benign-versus-malignant
discrimination.** Qualitative Doppler pattern (peripheral versus central/mixed
vascularity, presence of penetrating vessels) is a more consistently reported and more
directly comparable feature across studies (and is discussed further alongside
microvascular imaging in Section 5, since many of the studies reporting these patterns
use SMI or comparable slow-flow techniques rather than conventional color Doppler
alone).

### 4.4 Doppler in lymph node evaluation

In cervical lymph nodes generally (not restricted to the salivary-gland nodal basins
specifically), preserved hilar or "parallel" vascular branching is characteristic of
reactive nodes, while peripheral, subcapsular, or otherwise aberrant vascular
distribution raises concern for metastatic or lymphomatous involvement — a pattern
better characterized by microvascular imaging than by conventional Doppler in the
comparative literature available (see Section 5.4 and Section 13).

### 4.5 Summary appraisal

Qualitative color/power Doppler assessment (presence/absence and gross distribution of
flow) is a well-established, low-cost adjunct to grayscale imaging with genuine clinical
utility, particularly for inflammatory disease. Quantitative spectral Doppler cut-offs
for tumor characterization remain **promising but unvalidated**: supported by a small
number of studies with discordant findings and no confirmed pooled meta-analytic
estimate, and should not be presented to patients or in clinical reports as
diagnostically definitive.
## 5. Microvascular imaging

### 5.1 Principle

Microvascular imaging techniques — marketed under vendor-specific names including
Superb Microvascular Imaging (SMI, Canon/Toshiba), MicroFlow Imaging or MV-Flow
(Samsung), and comparable proprietary implementations from other manufacturers — use
adaptive clutter-suppression algorithms to separate genuine low-velocity blood flow
signal from tissue motion artifact without the wall-filter losses that limit
conventional color and power Doppler at low flow velocities. The result is
substantially greater sensitivity to slow flow in small-caliber vessels, without the
need for contrast agents. It is important not to conflate these as a single generic
technology: they are distinct proprietary implementations with different underlying
clutter-suppression algorithms, and cross-vendor comparative data for salivary gland
applications were not identified in this review — essentially all of the salivary-gland
microvascular-imaging literature identified here uses SMI specifically, and no
MicroFlow Imaging, MicroV, or other vendor-specific alternative was found applied to
this organ. Findings described below should therefore be understood as SMI-specific
until cross-vendor validation is available, not as generic "microvascular imaging"
findings.

### 5.2 Vascular pattern analysis in benign tumor subtyping

The most consistent application of SMI in this organ is differentiating pleomorphic
adenoma from Warthin tumor, a clinically meaningful distinction because management
differs (enucleation is often feasible for Warthin tumor, while pleomorphic adenoma
typically requires wider excision given its risk of recurrence and rare malignant
transformation). Multiple single- and dual-center studies report a consistent
directional pattern: pleomorphic adenoma tends to show sparse, peripheral, or avascular
distribution on SMI, while Warthin tumor tends to show more central or mixed, higher-
grade vascularity, with vessel density and a semiquantitative vascular-index score both
reported higher in Warthin tumor than pleomorphic adenoma (Ryoo et al., *J Ultrasound
Med* 2018, n = 62). Two additional, frequently cited 2020 studies report closely similar
cohort compositions, vascular-index terminology, and near-identical directional findings
for pleomorphic adenoma, Warthin tumor, and basal cell adenoma; whether these represent
genuinely independent confirmatory datasets or overlapping/duplicate reporting from the
same underlying cohort could not be resolved in this review and should be clarified by
the authors before either is cited as independent corroborating evidence.

### 5.3 Comparison with conventional Doppler

Across the studies identified, SMI is consistently reported as more sensitive than
conventional color or power Doppler flow imaging for depicting vessel number,
distribution, and branching pattern within salivary gland tumors, consistent with its
underlying technical rationale. However, **no completed systematic review or
meta-analysis of SMI diagnostic accuracy for salivary gland tumor characterization was
identified** — the only matching item found in this review's search was an apparently
unfulfilled 2021 review protocol, with no corresponding completed publication located.
Quantitative indices (vessel density, vascular index) vary in definition and threshold
across the available studies, and no cross-study pooled cut-off can currently be
recommended.

### 5.4 Adjacent evidence: lymph nodes and diffuse glandular disease

In cervical lymph nodes (not restricted to salivary-region basins), SMI has been
reported to outperform power Doppler for distinguishing benign from malignant nodes,
based on vessel branching pattern (hilar/parallel in benign nodes versus aberrant,
subcapsular, or dysplastic branching in malignant nodes), though this evidence is not
salivary-gland-specific and should be cited as adjacent, extrapolated evidence rather
than direct data (see Section 13). Within the salivary glands themselves, SMI-based
quantitative parenchymal vascularity indices have also been explored as a potential
adjunct in Sjögren's syndrome, where parenchymal vascularity is reported significantly
higher than in healthy controls — a diffuse-disease application distinct from, and less
mature than, its use in focal tumor characterization (see Section 10).

### 5.5 Summary appraisal

SMI/MVI for benign salivary tumor subtyping (particularly pleomorphic adenoma versus
Warthin tumor) is a **promising, mechanistically well-grounded but not yet formally
pooled or externally validated** application: directionally consistent across the
available single-center studies, technically more sensitive than conventional Doppler
by design, but lacking a completed systematic review, a resolved duplicate-publication
question, and any cross-vendor validation. It should currently be regarded as a useful
adjunct to grayscale and elastographic assessment rather than a stand-alone diagnostic
test, and its role in malignant-tumor subtype characterization specifically remains
essentially unstudied in the literature identified for this review.
## 6. Elastography

### 6.1 Strain elastography

Strain elastography derives a qualitative or semiquantitative measure of tissue
deformability from manual or physiological (e.g., pulsatile) compression, typically
displayed as a color-coded elasticity map and summarized either as a categorical
elasticity score or as a strain ratio between the lesion and adjacent reference tissue.
Its principal technical limitations are operator dependence in applying a controlled,
reproducible compression force, sensitivity to lesion depth (deeper lesions receive
attenuated and less uniform compression), and dependence on the composition of the
reference tissue chosen for the ratio, none of which are unique to the salivary glands
but all of which apply with particular force in this superficial but anatomically
variable organ.

### 6.2 Shear-wave elastography

Shear-wave elastography (2D-SWE, and point SWE where used) instead measures the
propagation velocity of acoustic radiation-force-induced shear waves through tissue,
reported either as a velocity (m/s) or converted to a Young's modulus-based stiffness
estimate (kPa), and is generally regarded as more operator-independent and quantitative
than strain elastography, though acquisition still requires attention to region-of-
interest (ROI) placement, minimal probe pressure, adequate acoustic window, and, per
general SWE methodology, multiple acquisitions with a reported median or mean value
rather than a single measurement.

### 6.3 Benign versus malignant tumor differentiation: an instructive case of biological and technical heterogeneity

Shear-wave elastography of salivary gland tumors is one of the clearest illustrations in
this entire review of why a single universal cut-off is not achievable, for reasons that
are partly technical and partly biological. In an early, influential series (Bhatia et
al., *Eur Radiol* 2012, n = 60 lesions), median shear modulus was, counter to intuition,
*lower* in malignant lesions (13.5 kPa) than in benign lesions (18.3 kPa) as a group,
because pleomorphic adenoma — the most common benign tumor — can itself be markedly
stiff (median 22.5 kPa, versus 16.9 kPa for Warthin tumor) owing to its myxochondroid
stromal component, while two mucoepidermoid carcinomas in the same series were
substantial stiffness outliers (81.9 and 132.0 kPa). This is not merely inter-study
noise: it is a stated, mechanistically explained biological confound — a benign tumor
subtype can be stiffer than some malignant tumors — meaning that even a perfectly
reproducible, vendor-independent SWE measurement could not, in principle, cleanly
separate benign from malignant salivary tumors using stiffness alone.

Consistent with this, two systematic reviews/meta-analyses addressing ostensibly the
same clinical question — elastography (strain and/or shear-wave, pooled) for benign-
versus-malignant salivary tumor differentiation — report substantially different pooled
estimates: a 2022 meta-analysis of 16 studies (1,105 patients) reported pooled
sensitivity 0.73 and specificity 0.64 (Wu et al., *Front Oncol* 2022), while a 2025
meta-analysis of 26 studies (1,609 patients) reported pooled sensitivity 0.75 and
specificity 0.79 (*BMC Oral Health* 2025) — an approximately 15-percentage-point gap in
pooled specificity for nominally the same question, three years apart, with different
(though overlapping) primary-study sets. The earlier meta-analysis explicitly attributes
between-study heterogeneity chiefly to sonoelastographic measurement technique
(qualitative score versus semiquantitative strain ratio versus quantitative SWE), noting
that quantitative and semiquantitative methods outperformed purely qualitative scoring.
Taken together, the biological confound described above and this magnitude of
inter-meta-analysis discordance mean that **no single kPa or m/s cut-off for benign-
versus-malignant salivary tumor differentiation can currently be recommended**, and any
reported cut-off value in an individual primary study should be understood as
local/vendor/technique-specific rather than generalizable.

### 6.4 Elastography in radiation-induced fibrosis

A smaller but directionally consistent body of evidence supports SWE as a marker of
post-radiotherapy salivary gland fibrosis: parotid and submandibular stiffness have been
reported significantly higher in irradiated glands than in healthy controls in more than
one small case-control study, correlating in at least one series with reduced salivary
flow and xerostomia severity. This is a mechanistically plausible, diffuse-disease
application distinct from focal tumor characterization, and while the evidence base is
still limited to small, largely single-center studies, the direction of effect is
consistent across the studies identified.

### 6.5 Elastography in Sjögren's syndrome

This is the most consistently supported diagnostic elastography application identified
in this review. At least three independent systematic reviews or meta-analyses were
published within an approximately 18-month window (2023–2024) addressing salivary gland
elastography in Sjögren's syndrome or sicca symptoms, of which the most detailed
retrievable pooled estimate reports 15 studies (816 patients, 735 controls) with pooled
sensitivity 0.80 and specificity 0.87, with formal meta-regression identifying country,
diagnostic criteria, vendor/imaging system, specific SWE technique, measurement
location, control-group definition, and patient age as plausible sources of
heterogeneity (*Eur Radiol* 2023). That three separate, near-contemporaneous systematic
reviews addressed the same question is itself informative: it suggests the primary
evidence base, while reasonably consistent in pooled sensitivity/specificity, is still
regarded by investigators as unsettled enough to warrant repeated re-synthesis, and no
single elastography value should be presented as a stand-alone diagnostic threshold for
Sjögren's syndrome independent of the semiquantitative OMERACT grey-scale framework
discussed in Section 10, with which several recent studies have explicitly attempted
combination.

### 6.6 Summary appraisal

Elastography's most defensible current clinical role is as a *contributory*, not
stand-alone, parameter: promising for benign tumor subtyping and reasonably well
supported (pooled sensitivity/specificity in the 0.80s) as an adjunct in Sjögren's
syndrome, but explicitly **not supported by the evidence for a universal cut-off in
benign-versus-malignant tumor differentiation**, for reasons that are partly a genuine
biological limit (pleomorphic adenoma stiffness) and partly a matter of unresolved
technique and vendor heterogeneity. Reviews and clinical protocols that present a single
numeric elastography threshold for malignancy risk without qualifying vendor and
technique should be read with this heterogeneity explicitly in mind.
## 7. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS)

### 7.1 Rationale and technique

CEUS relies on intravenously administered microbubble contrast agents (e.g., sulfur
hexafluoride-based agents, marketed as SonoVue in Europe/Asia and as Lumason in North
America) imaged with low mechanical-index, contrast-specific pulse-sequence techniques
that selectively detect the nonlinear harmonic signal generated by the microbubbles
while suppressing tissue background signal. This provides real-time, radiation-free
depiction of microvascular perfusion at the arterial (early) and later phases without
the nephrotoxicity concerns associated with iodinated or gadolinium-based contrast
agents, an advantage of particular relevance in patients with renal impairment or
contrast allergy who might otherwise require CT or MRI for tissue characterization.
Dedicated salivary-gland dosing protocols distinct from general non-hepatic CEUS
practice were not identified in this review; dosing appears to follow general
superficial-organ CEUS convention rather than a salivary-gland-specific validated
protocol.

### 7.2 Enhancement patterns and their reported associations

Across the comparative studies identified, homogeneous or "slow-in" enhancement
patterns are more often reported in pleomorphic adenoma, while Warthin tumor more often
shows a "slow-out" pattern and a tendency toward greater capillary density; malignant
lesions are more often described as showing blurred or ill-defined enhancement margins,
heterogeneous texture, and a "fast-in, fast-out" wash-in/wash-out pattern, with overlap
against benign lesions — particularly pleomorphic adenoma, which can mimic a
malignant-appearing pattern on grayscale/Doppler assessment alone — explicitly noted in
more than one source. These qualitative pattern descriptions are broadly consistent
across studies but, as with grayscale signs, should not be treated as individually
sufficient for diagnosis.

### 7.3 Quantitative CEUS and time-intensity curve analysis

Quantitative perfusion software (most consistently, VueBox-type external
time-intensity-curve analysis in the studies identified) extracts parameters including
arrival/rise time, time to peak (TTP), peak enhancement/intensity, wash-in rate or
gradient, area under the curve (AUC), and mean transit time (MTT, sometimes normalized
as nMTT). The best-supported quantitative finding in this domain is a 2022 systematic
review and meta-analysis of 15 studies specifically addressing pleomorphic adenoma
versus Warthin tumor differentiation, which reported pooled normalized mean transit time
significantly longer in pleomorphic adenoma (1.45 vs 0.62) and pooled time to peak
significantly longer in pleomorphic adenoma (26.9 s vs 16.9 s), alongside more
frequently heterogeneous enhancement texture in pleomorphic adenoma; enhanced lesion
size, enhancement margin, and enhancement rim did not differ significantly between the
two tumor types in this pooled analysis (Rogalska et al., *J Clin Med* 2022). This is a
genuinely well-evidenced, multiply-reproduced quantitative CEUS finding for this specific
differential — but it should be noted explicitly that **this pooled analysis addresses
differentiation between two benign tumor types, not malignancy detection**: no
comparably robust pooled sensitivity/specificity estimate for CEUS distinguishing
malignant from benign salivary gland tumors as a whole was identified in this review,
and the evidence base for that specific, arguably more clinically urgent question rests
on single-center cohort studies rather than pooled meta-analysis.

### 7.4 Comparison with other techniques

Direct head-to-head comparisons of CEUS against MRI for salivary gland lesion
characterization were not identified as having been performed; available comparative
literature instead pairs CEUS against grayscale/Doppler US and, less commonly, against
strain elastography within the same cohort, with combined multiparametric use (e.g.,
strain elastography plus CEUS) reported to modestly improve area-under-the-curve
performance over either technique alone in at least one single-center study, though the
full bibliographic details of that study could not be independently confirmed for this
review and its figures should be treated as provisional pending direct verification. As
with elastography and microvascular imaging, CEUS is best framed as a contributory
technique within a multiparametric assessment rather than a stand-alone replacement for
either conventional US or cross-sectional imaging.

### 7.5 Guidelines, standardization, and regulatory status

The EFSUMB non-hepatic CEUS guideline update (Sidhu et al., *Ultraschall Med* 2018)
plausibly includes salivary glands among its superficial-organ applications, but this
review was unable to independently verify from primary text whether the guideline
contains explicit, salivary-gland-specific recommendations, enhancement-pattern
nomenclature, or graded statements, and this should be confirmed directly by the authors
before the manuscript asserts specific guideline content. No dedicated,
salivary-gland-specific CEUS position statement or consensus document from any
international ultrasound society was identified. No salivary-gland-specific CEUS
reproducibility, inter-observer, or inter-vendor/inter-software validation study was
identified — a real gap, given that the quantitative studies reviewed above rely on a
specific external perfusion-analysis software package, and cross-software comparability
for this organ has not, on the evidence available, been formally tested. The regulatory
(on-label versus off-label) status of currently available microbubble agents for
salivary gland use could not be confirmed from primary product labeling within the
constraints of this review and should not be stated definitively without independent
confirmation.

One retracted publication in this space (a 2022 *Journal of Healthcare Engineering*
article on combined CEUS and elastography for salivary tumor differentiation, retracted
by the publisher in 2023 following an investigation into systematic manipulation of the
publication process) was identified during this review's literature search and should
not be cited as supporting evidence in the manuscript or in any future work drawing on
this domain.

### 7.6 Summary appraisal

CEUS is **well supported for pleomorphic adenoma-versus-Warthin tumor differentiation**
via quantitative time-intensity curve parameters, drawing on a genuine, pooled
meta-analytic evidence base. It is **promising but not yet validated by pooled evidence
for malignancy detection** as a general discrimination task, and formal
society-level guidance, reproducibility data, and regulatory clarity specific to this
organ remain gaps that should temper how confidently this technique is presented in
clinical protocols.
## 8. Multiparametric ultrasound: an integration framework

Sections 3–7 examined each ultrasound domain in isolation because that is how the
evidence base is structured — the overwhelming majority of studies identified in this
review test one technique, or at most two, against a single reference standard, rather
than testing a pre-specified combination of all five domains as a single index test. It
is therefore important to be explicit about a distinction that is easy to blur: there is
a meaningful difference between *clinicians combining several techniques' output
qualitatively during interpretation* (well represented in the literature, e.g., Wakonig
et al.'s quantitative multiparametric protocols combining B-mode, elastography, and
CEUS) and a *formally validated, pre-specified multiparametric diagnostic algorithm*
with its own prospectively tested accuracy (essentially unrepresented in the literature
identified for this review). Most of what is described as "multiparametric ultrasound"
in the current salivary gland literature is the former, not the latter.

With that distinction stated, the following framework reflects how the techniques in
Sections 3–7 can be layered onto one another in clinical practice, in order of
increasing complexity, cost, and evidentiary maturity:

**B-mode** establishes the presence, location, and gross morphology of a lesion or
diffuse process and generates the initial differential diagnosis (Section 3). **Color/
power Doppler** adds a first, low-cost read on vascularity, most useful for
distinguishing active inflammation from quiescent disease and for a coarse read on
tumor vascularity, albeit with acknowledged limits for benign-versus-malignant
discrimination (Section 4). **Microvascular imaging (SMI/MVI)**, where available, adds
finer-grained vascular architecture information, currently best supported for
pleomorphic adenoma-versus-Warthin tumor subtyping (Section 5). **Elastography** adds
mechanical (stiffness) information that is genuinely informative for benign tumor
subtyping and, particularly, for Sjögren's syndrome, while carrying an important
biological caveat that limits its use for malignancy exclusion (Section 6). **CEUS**
adds perfusion-kinetic information, well supported for the same pleomorphic
adenoma-versus-Warthin tumor question and promising, though less robustly pooled, for
broader malignancy assessment (Section 7). **Tissue sampling** (Section 14) remains the
step that resolves diagnostic uncertainty that imaging — at any level of
sophistication — cannot resolve on its own, and should not be deferred indefinitely in
pursuit of a non-invasive multiparametric diagnosis when clinical concern for
malignancy is significant.

This ordering reflects typical acquisition sequence and increasing technical/resource
burden, not a validated diagnostic algorithm with its own prospectively measured
accuracy: **no study identified in this review formally tested a pre-specified
combination of all five domains against a single reference standard with pooled
diagnostic-accuracy reporting for the combination as a whole.** The closest available
evidence — studies combining, for example, elastography with CEUS, or SWE with
grayscale-based prediction models — do report incremental improvement in area-under-
the-curve performance over single-technique assessment in the specific cohorts studied,
which is a reasonable basis for clinical practice, but should not be over-generalized
into a claim that multiparametric ultrasound as a composite test has been formally
validated. The workflow proposed in Section 16 of this review should accordingly be read
as **the authors' structured clinical proposal, explicitly not a validated algorithm or
society guideline**, built from the technique-by-technique evidence summarized above and
intended to make that evidence usable at the point of care while being transparent about
where it rests on consolidated data and where it rests on extrapolation and clinical
judgment.
## 9. Inflammatory and obstructive diseases

### 9.1 Acute, chronic, and recurrent sialadenitis

Ultrasound is an established first-line tool for inflammatory and obstructive salivary
gland disease, reported useful in the large majority of presenting cases in at least one
structured review of the literature combined with institutional experience, though the
authors of that review note that robust comparative-accuracy data are considerably more
developed for obstructive disease (sialolithiasis, tumors) than for pure, non-obstructive
inflammatory disease (Koch et al., *J Clin Med* 2021). Acute sialadenitis typically shows
glandular enlargement with hypoechoic, heterogeneous parenchyma, hypo- or anechoic foci
where microabscess formation is present, ductal dilation in bacterial/purulent forms, and
increased color/power Doppler flow (hyperemia). Chronic sialadenitis instead shows
heterogeneous echogenicity reflecting parenchymal fibrosis, ductal dilation, and
glandular atrophy, typically with reduced rather than increased vascularity. Juvenile
recurrent parotitis produces a distinctive scattered pattern of multiple small
hypoechoic foci — commonly described as a "Swiss cheese" or "moth-eaten" appearance —
with hypervascularity during acute episodes and reduced vascularity between episodes;
sialendoscopy has an established therapeutic as well as diagnostic role in this
condition. Advanced techniques (elastography, CEUS) have not, on the evidence
identified in this review, been systematically studied for non-obstructive sialadenitis
specifically, in contrast to their more developed evidence base in Sjögren's syndrome
(Section 10) — a gap worth stating explicitly.

### 9.2 Sialolithiasis

Ultrasound diagnosis of sialolithiasis is one of the best-evidenced diagnostic
applications in this entire review. A 2022 meta-analysis of 10 studies (1,393 patients)
using sialendoscopy, sialography, or surgery as reference standards reported a pooled
diagnostic odds ratio of 162.6 and a summary receiver-operating-characteristic AUC of
0.96, with parotid-gland calculi detected somewhat more accurately than submandibular
calculi in subgroup analysis (Kim et al., *Laryngoscope* 2022). Individual comparative
studies against direct stone identification or surgical reference standards report
sensitivities in the 77–95% range and specificities in the 95–97% range (Terraz et al.,
*AJR* 2013; Goncalves et al., *J Ultrasound Med* 2017), though at least one more recent
comparative study reported internally inconsistent sensitivity figures for ultrasound
within its own published synthesis (65% versus 86%, depending on which part of the
study's results are cited), a discrepancy that should be resolved against the primary
text rather than silently picked before being used in any future citation. Classic
sonographic findings are an echogenic focus with posterior acoustic shadowing and
proximal ductal dilation; small or purely intraductal stones, and stones located deep
within the distal Wharton's duct obscured by mandibular shadowing, remain recognized
sources of false-negative examination.

### 9.3 Ductal stenosis

Ultrasound can identify pre-stenotic ductal dilation and localize the general
transition point of a suspected stricture, but a normal-caliber duct on ultrasound does
not exclude stenosis, and sialendoscopy remains the reference standard for definitive
diagnosis, characterization, and — commonly in the same procedure — therapeutic
management of ductal strictures, with gland preservation achieved in the majority of
appropriately selected sialendoscopy cases per the general sialendoscopy literature.
Dedicated, high-quality primary literature specifically on the diagnostic accuracy of
ultrasound for ductal stenosis (as distinct from sialolithiasis) is comparatively
sparse and largely descriptive rather than accuracy-focused — an explicit gap.

### 9.4 Abscess

Salivary gland abscess typically appears as a heterogeneous fluid collection with an
anechoic or hypoechoic center and an isoechoic-to-hyperechoic inflammatory rim, with
increased peripheral vascularity on Doppler, in contrast to the homogeneous, mildly
hyperechoic appearance of normal parenchyma. Ultrasound-guided needle aspiration is
reported as a safe, effective, less invasive alternative to surgical incision and
drainage for parotid abscess in at least one comparative study, albeit with a higher
average number of repeat procedures required (approximately 1.9 aspirations versus 1.1
surgical interventions per patient in that series) — a trade-off between invasiveness
and procedural efficiency that should be discussed with the patient rather than
presented as a straightforwardly superior option in every case (see also Section 14.6).

### 9.5 Summary appraisal

Ultrasound diagnosis of sialolithiasis is **consolidated, high-accuracy evidence** —
among the strongest diagnostic claims in this review. Grayscale-and-Doppler diagnosis
of sialadenitis in its various forms is **well established descriptively** but rests on
a comparatively thinner formal-accuracy evidence base, and ductal stenosis assessment by
ultrasound alone is **an adjunct, not a substitute, for sialendoscopy**. Advanced
multiparametric techniques remain largely unstudied for this disease category outside
the Sjögren's-syndrome-specific literature reviewed in Section 10.
## 10. Autoimmune and systemic diseases

### 10.1 Sjögren's syndrome: the OMERACT salivary gland ultrasound score

Salivary gland ultrasonography (SGUS) in Sjögren's syndrome is, on the evidence
assembled for this review, the single most rigorously developed and validated advanced
application of salivary gland ultrasound of any disease category considered in this
manuscript, and is treated in correspondingly greater depth here.

The Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (OMERACT) ultrasound working group has, across a
sequence of consensus and reliability publications beginning with a 2017 core-item
consensus definition, developed a semiquantitative 0–3 grey-scale scoring system based
principally on parenchymal inhomogeneity of the parotid and submandibular glands, with
grades 0–1 considered normal and 2–3 pathological (Jousse-Joulin et al., *RMD Open*
2017; *Ann Rheum Dis* 2019). A subsequent OMERACT reliability exercise extended this
work toward incorporating glandular vascularization/inflammation scoring (Jousse-Joulin
et al., *Rheumatology* 2022). Reported diagnostic performance of the 2019 OMERACT score
against 2016 ACR/EULAR classification criteria is good, with sensitivity in the low-70%
range and specificity above 90% in at least one comparative study using a total-score
cut-off in at least one gland (Robin et al., *Arthritis Care Res* 2022); a larger pooled
meta-analytic estimate across multiple scoring systems reports sensitivities in the
75–84% range and specificities in the 88–95% range with a summary AUC of 0.93–0.95
(*Scientific Reports* 2018).

**Reproducibility** deserves explicit, honest treatment rather than a single quoted
figure. OMERACT reliability exercises using structured training conditions (video
clips, workshops) report good to excellent inter- and intra-observer agreement; a
dedicated atlas-based pilot study found that, without a reference atlas, inter- and
intra-reader reliability were low, improving to moderate (mean kappa 0.52, range
0.31–0.77) for inter-reader and good (mean kappa 0.69, range 0.46–0.86) for intra-reader
agreement once a reference atlas was introduced, and a subsequent international web-based
training initiative similarly aimed at improving reliability among sonographers of
varying experience levels. **The honest synthesis is that reliability of the OMERACT
grey-scale score is good-to-excellent for experienced or specifically trained readers
under structured/exercise conditions, and demonstrably improves further with reference
materials or formal training — but is more modest among less experienced readers or in
unstructured real-world practice**, and reliability for individual sub-items
(echogenicity, homogeneity grading) is generally lower than for overall gestalt
scoring. This nuance should be represented plainly rather than citing a single
reliability figure as if it applied universally across all reader experience levels and
practice settings.

**Correlation with disease activity and biopsy** is inconsistent across the literature
identified: reported correlations between SGUS score and the clinical ESSDAI activity
index range from strong in some cohorts to statistically non-significant in others, and
concordance between SGUS score and minor salivary gland biopsy (focus score) has been
reported at roughly two-thirds agreement in at least one single-center cohort, with
sensitivity and specificity of the SGUS score against biopsy in the moderate range in
that same study. This heterogeneity across cohorts should be represented as such rather
than resolved into a single correlation coefficient. Current EULAR-associated management
guidance (per this review's search, not independently confirmed against primary text and
flagged for verification before citation) reportedly frames SGUS as relevant chiefly to
lymphoma-risk stratification in patients with specific risk features (elevated
cryoglobulins, salivary gland mass, persistent lymphadenopathy, unexplained systemic
symptoms, or high ESSDAI), rather than as a routine screening tool for all patients with
suspected Sjögren's syndrome.

**Quantitative techniques beyond grey-scale scoring** — Doppler-based parenchymal
vascularity, SMI-based vascularity indices, and 2D-SWE — have each been explored as
adjuncts, generally showing significantly higher vascularity or stiffness in Sjögren's
syndrome patients than in healthy controls, and combined grey-scale-plus-SWE approaches
have been reported to modestly improve area-under-the-curve performance over grey-scale
scoring alone in at least one study. These remain adjunctive and less validated than the
core grey-scale OMERACT score itself.

### 10.2 IgG4-related disease

IgG4-related sialadenitis (including Mikulicz disease, with bilateral lacrimal and
salivary gland involvement, and Küttner tumor, typically an isolated submandibular
presentation) shows a sonographic pattern of multiple hypoechoic areas of varying size
within symmetrically enlarged glands, with increased vascularity, that has been reported
useful in differentiating this entity from Sjögren's syndrome in at least one dedicated
comparative imaging study (Shimizu et al., *Arthritis Res Ther* 2015). Overlap with
Sjögren-pattern sonographic change (multiple hypoechoic zones, glandular inhomogeneity)
is explicitly acknowledged as a diagnostic challenge in the literature identified, and
the evidence base, while real, is considerably thinner than for Sjögren's syndrome —
limited to a small number of studies, largely single-center.

### 10.3 Sarcoidosis

Salivary gland involvement in sarcoidosis is sonographically nonspecific (hypoechoic
nodules, occasional microcalcification, increased vascularity, overlapping with chronic
sialadenitis and other granulomatous disease) and the evidence base identified for this
review is essentially confined to case reports and general head-and-neck imaging
reviews; **no validated sarcoidosis-specific sonographic scoring system was identified**,
and this should be stated as a genuine evidence gap rather than implied to be
comparably developed to the Sjögren's-syndrome literature.

### 10.4 HIV-related salivary gland disease

HIV-associated salivary gland disease, spanning diffuse infiltrative lymphocytosis
syndrome (DILS) and benign lymphoepithelial cysts/lesions, produces a sonographic
spectrum from diffuse lymphocytic infiltration with multiple small cysts (the classic
pre-antiretroviral-therapy pattern) to solid or mixed cystic-solid nodules, with reported
low-resistance arterial flow in mixed nodules and explicitly nonspecific appearance,
particularly for mixed lesions that can mimic neoplasm. The evidence base is largely
older (1990s–2000s case series) with a shift in presentation pattern reported since the
introduction of combination antiretroviral therapy; **no OMERACT-type validated scoring
system exists for this indication**, and modern, large systematic cohort data in the
post-antiretroviral-therapy era were not identified in this review.

### 10.5 Summary appraisal

The OMERACT grey-scale SGUS score for Sjögren's syndrome is **the most validated
advanced sonographic scoring system addressed in this entire review**, with the
important caveat that its reliability is training- and experience-dependent rather than
uniformly excellent across all readers and settings. IgG4-related disease sonography is
a **real but comparatively thin evidence base**. Sarcoidosis and HIV-related salivary
gland disease sonography remain, on current evidence, **descriptive and case-level**
rather than validated diagnostic applications.
## 11. Benign focal lesions and tumors

### 11.1 Pleomorphic adenoma

The most common salivary gland tumor overall, pleomorphic adenoma typically presents
sonographically as a well-defined, lobulated, homogeneously hypoechoic mass with
posterior acoustic enhancement, predominantly peripheral or sparse (grade 0–1)
vascularity on color Doppler and microvascular imaging, and — the pattern most
distinctive of this tumor among all techniques reviewed — a stiffness on shear-wave
elastography that can be high and can overlap with or exceed that of some malignant
tumors, owing to its myxochondroid stromal component (Section 6.3). On CEUS,
pleomorphic adenoma more often shows homogeneous or "slow-in" enhancement with a
comparatively long time to peak and normalized mean transit time relative to Warthin
tumor (Section 7.3). Cystic change is comparatively uncommon (reported in a small
minority of cases in comparative series) and, when marked, should prompt reconsideration
of the diagnosis rather than being assumed typical.

### 11.2 Warthin tumor

Warthin tumor (papillary cystadenoma lymphomatosum) is the second most common benign
parotid tumor, characteristically multiple or bilateral in a meaningful minority of
cases, and typically shows a more heterogeneous, often partly cystic appearance with
anechoic components reported in a substantial proportion of cases, in contrast to
pleomorphic adenoma. Vascularity is characteristically more central and higher-grade
(grade 2–3) on color Doppler and microvascular imaging than pleomorphic adenoma
(Section 5.2), and CEUS more often shows a "slow-out" pattern with a shorter time to
peak and normalized mean transit time than pleomorphic adenoma (Section 7.3).
Elastographic stiffness is generally, though not invariably, lower than pleomorphic
adenoma. Because enucleation is often oncologically adequate for Warthin tumor while
pleomorphic adenoma typically warrants wider excision, the pleomorphic
adenoma-versus-Warthin tumor distinction is the single most clinically actionable, and
best evidence-supported, benign-tumor discrimination task addressed by mpUS in this
review — supported convergently by microvascular imaging, elastography, and CEUS, even
though each technique individually carries the caveats detailed in Sections 5–7.

### 11.3 Oncocytoma, basal cell adenoma, lipoma, and cysts

Oncocytoma appears sonographically as a hypoechoic, well-defined mass without features
that reliably distinguish it from other benign parotid tumors — a genuine grayscale
overlap problem repeatedly noted in the literature identified. Basal cell adenoma, the
next most common benign parotid tumor after pleomorphic adenoma and Warthin tumor in
most series, may show cystic degeneration, though the primary reports of this finding
in the literature identified were not confirmed as ultrasound-based (imaging modality
was more often CT or MRI in the retrieved sources) and this should not be presented as
established sonographic knowledge without further verification. **No dedicated,
well-characterized primary ultrasound-morphology study for basal cell adenoma, lipoma,
or simple/benign salivary cysts was identified in this review** — a genuine gap that
should be stated plainly rather than filled with extrapolation from general soft-tissue
ultrasound principles (e.g., that lipomas are typically well-defined, mildly hyperechoic
to isoechoic, compressible masses, or that simple cysts are anechoic with posterior
enhancement, both standard general-ultrasound expectations not specifically validated
for this organ in the literature reviewed).

### 11.4 Benign lymphoepithelial lesions

Benign lymphoepithelial cysts and lesions, most classically associated with HIV
infection (Section 10.4), show a wide morphologic spectrum from simple cyst to mixed
solid-cystic mass, with mixed lesions showing greater vascularity and low-resistance
arterial flow than purely cystic lesions in the small body of literature identified;
sonographic appearance is explicitly described as nonspecific, particularly for the
mixed solid-cystic pattern, which can resemble neoplasm.

### 11.5 Summary appraisal

The pleomorphic adenoma-versus-Warthin tumor distinction is **the best-supported
multiparametric application among all benign focal lesions**, convergently informed by
grayscale morphology, microvascular imaging, elastography, and CEUS. Beyond this
specific pair, primary ultrasound-morphology evidence for the remaining common benign
tumor types (oncocytoma, basal cell adenoma, lipoma, cysts) is **sparse or absent**, and
this review explicitly declines to present confident sonographic differentiating
criteria for these entities where the underlying literature does not support them.
## 12. Malignant salivary gland tumors

### 12.1 General limitations of ultrasound for histologic classification

Malignant salivary gland tumors — mucoepidermoid carcinoma, adenoid cystic carcinoma,
acinic cell carcinoma, salivary duct carcinoma, carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma,
metastatic disease, and lymphoma among others — share, on grayscale and Doppler
ultrasound, a broadly overlapping appearance of hypoechogenicity, variable margin
definition, heterogeneous echotexture, and variably increased vascularity, without
reliable sonographic features that predict specific histology. This is a structural
limitation, not a gap that better technique alone will close: ultrasound characterizes
gross tissue architecture and perfusion, not the cytological and molecular features that
define histologic subtype, and this review found **no dedicated, confirmable
primary-literature study establishing histotype-specific grayscale or Doppler
sonographic signatures for mucoepidermoid carcinoma, adenoid cystic carcinoma, acinic
cell carcinoma, salivary duct carcinoma, or carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma**
specifically, as distinct from generic "malignant salivary mass" features. Diagnostic
literature addressing these specific histotypes is predominantly cytology-based (see
Section 14.2, the Milan System), not sonomorphology-based. Carcinoma ex pleomorphic
adenoma is best suspected from clinical history — a long-standing parotid mass with
recent rapid growth, pain, or new facial nerve palsy — rather than from a specific
sonographic sign.

### 12.2 What ultrasound can and cannot tell the clinician about malignancy risk

As established in Section 3, conventional grayscale-and-Doppler assessment is
better at ruling malignancy *in* (high specificity, in the 90–95% range across the
comparative studies identified) than ruling it *out* (moderate sensitivity, commonly in
the 60–66% range), meaning a substantial minority of malignant tumors present with a
reassuringly benign-appearing conventional ultrasound examination. This
specificity-over-sensitivity pattern recurs across the advanced techniques reviewed in
Sections 4–7 as well — elastography and CEUS add diagnostically useful information in
specific comparisons (particularly benign tumor subtyping) but no technique reviewed
here, alone or in the combinations actually tested in the literature, achieves
sensitivity high enough to safely exclude malignancy without tissue sampling in a
clinically concerning lesion. This is a central, recurring message of this review and
should directly inform the workflow proposed in Section 16: **a benign-appearing
multiparametric ultrasound examination reduces, but does not eliminate, the probability
of malignancy**, and the threshold for tissue sampling should be set accordingly rather
than deferred in reliance on a reassuring scan.

### 12.3 Signs of local infiltration, perineural spread, and extraglandular extension

Infiltrative growth into adjacent muscle or loss of the normal tissue-plane interface is
a grayscale feature that, when clearly present, raises substantial concern for
malignancy, though its absence does not exclude malignancy given the overlap described
above. **Ultrasound has no meaningful, direct role in the assessment of perineural
tumor spread** — a well-recognized feature of adenoid cystic carcinoma in particular —
because this requires visualization of nerve fascicles and foraminal/skull-base
anatomy well beyond the depth and field of view accessible to ultrasound; this
assessment is the specific province of MRI, and this limitation should be stated
plainly wherever perineural spread is a clinical concern, rather than left implicit.
Similarly, deep-lobe or parapharyngeal extraglandular extension is frequently
underestimated or entirely missed by ultrasound because of acoustic shadowing from the
mandible, and is again an indication for MRI rather than more advanced ultrasound
technique (see Section 16 for when cross-sectional imaging is required regardless of US
findings).

### 12.4 Metastatic disease and lymphoma

Metastasis to the salivary glands, most commonly the parotid via intraparotid lymph
nodes, arises predominantly from head-and-neck cutaneous primaries (cutaneous squamous
cell carcinoma of the scalp and face being the most frequently cited source), with
sonographic features of the involved node(s) — solid, hypoechoic, ill-defined margins,
a tendency toward central necrosis, occasionally multiple involved nodes — generally
following the same reasoning applied to metastatic cervical lymphadenopathy elsewhere
(Section 13). Salivary gland (typically MALT-type) lymphoma, which can arise as a
complication of longstanding Sjögren's syndrome, has been described with a "linear
echogenic strands" or multiple small hypoechoic nodule pattern in a segmental
distribution, though this review was unable to identify a well-characterized primary
citation specifically substantiating this descriptor for salivary parenchymal (as
opposed to nodal) lymphoma, and this should be regarded as a provisional descriptive
pattern pending better primary-source support.

### 12.5 Summary appraisal

Ultrasound, at any level of technical sophistication reviewed in this manuscript, is
**better at raising suspicion of malignancy than at excluding it, and has essentially no
established role in predicting specific malignant histotype or in assessing perineural
spread or deep extraglandular extension**. These are structural, not merely
evidence-immature, limitations, and should shape how confidently any ultrasound report
— conventional or multiparametric — is worded when malignancy is in the differential.
## 13. Lymph nodes

Evaluation of intraparotid and adjacent cervical lymph nodes is an integral part of a
complete salivary gland ultrasound examination, relevant to inflammatory, autoimmune,
and neoplastic disease alike. Much of the evidence supporting nodal characterization
criteria derives from the general cervical lymph node literature rather than from
studies restricted to the salivary-region nodal basins specifically, and this should be
understood as adjacent, extrapolated evidence rather than organ-specific data
throughout this section.

**Morphology.** Reactive (benign) nodes are typically oval, with a preserved echogenic
fatty hilum, an indistinct or gently sloping border, no necrosis, and central or hilar
vascularity on Doppler and microvascular imaging. Metastatic nodes tend to be rounder,
show loss or effacement of the echogenic hilum, have sharper borders, and may show
intranodal necrosis or calcification, with peripheral or otherwise aberrant vascular
distribution. Loss of the echogenic hilum is reported as the single most sensitive sign
of malignant involvement, while the short-axis-to-long-axis ratio is reported as the
most specific single parameter — but neither is sufficient in isolation, and an
important overlap caveat is explicitly documented in the literature: metastatic,
lymphomatous, and even tuberculous nodes can retain a visible echogenic hilum early in
the course of involvement, before medullary architecture is disrupted, meaning **hilar
preservation does not reliably exclude malignant or lymphomatous involvement**.

**Size criteria.** No single universal short-axis size cut-off reliably distinguishes
benign from neoplastic cervical nodes, a limitation explicitly acknowledged in the
literature reviewed; a general teaching threshold of greater than 1 cm short axis as
"abnormal" is widely used but should be applied as one contributory criterion among
several (morphology, hilum, vascularity), not as a stand-alone rule.

**Microvascular imaging and elastography.** SMI has been reported, in the general
cervical lymph node literature, to outperform power Doppler for distinguishing benign
from malignant nodes based on vessel branching pattern (Section 5.4), and this principle
is reasonably extrapolated to intraparotid and periglandular nodes, though
salivary-region-specific validation data are limited. Dedicated elastography and CEUS
evidence specifically for intraparotid or periglandular (as opposed to generic cervical)
lymph node characterization was not identified as a distinct body of literature in this
review and should be regarded as an extrapolated, not directly validated, application.

**Summary appraisal.** Nodal characterization criteria used in the salivary gland
literature are, on the evidence available, **largely inherited from the general
cervical lymph node literature rather than independently validated for the
intraparotid/periglandular nodal basin specifically**. This should be stated candidly:
the reasoning is sound and widely applied in practice, but organ-specific validation
data are limited.
## 14. Interventional ultrasound

### 14.1 US-guided fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC)

Ultrasound-guided FNA is a well-established, low-morbidity first-line sampling technique
for salivary gland lesions, with reported sensitivity and specificity that vary widely
across single-institution series (roughly 60–100% sensitivity and 90–100% specificity
across the range of studies identified in this review), reflecting differences in
operator experience, aspirate adequacy, and case mix rather than a single stable
performance figure. **No large-scale, multi-institutional meta-analysis specifically
quantifying pooled sensitivity/specificity of ultrasound-guided (as opposed to blind or
palpation-guided) FNA for salivary gland lesions** was identified with confirmed pooled
numbers; the wide reported range above should be understood as a synthesis across
heterogeneous single-institution series, not a validated pooled estimate.

### 14.2 The Milan System for Reporting Salivary Gland Cytopathology

The Milan System for Reporting Salivary Gland Cytopathology (MSRSGC), first published in
2018 under the sponsorship of the American Society of Cytopathology and the
International Academy of Cytology, provides a standardized six-tiered diagnostic
category framework (non-diagnostic; non-neoplastic; atypia of undetermined
significance, AUS; benign neoplasm; salivary gland neoplasm of uncertain malignant
potential, SUMP; suspicious for malignancy; malignant), each associated with an
estimated implied risk of malignancy intended to guide management. A second edition,
co-published simultaneously across three cytopathology journals in 2023/2024, refined
risk-of-malignancy estimates using accumulated systematic-review/meta-analysis evidence,
added imaging guidance, and updated ancillary/molecular testing recommendations while
retaining the original six-tier structure. **No further update beyond this 2023/2024
second edition was identified as published through 2026.**

Institutional validation studies applying the Milan System report **substantial
inter-institutional heterogeneity in observed risk-of-malignancy figures**, particularly
for the lower-volume, diagnostically ambiguous categories — AUS and SUMP — where
reported risk of malignancy has ranged, across different single-center series identified
in this review, from roughly one-half to values considerably higher or lower depending
on institution and case mix. This heterogeneity is a genuine, acknowledged limitation of
the system as currently implemented, not merely a reporting artifact, and should be
represented as such: the Milan System standardizes terminology and reporting structure
effectively, but the specific risk-of-malignancy percentage attached to each category by
any individual institution should be interpreted with its own local validation data in
mind rather than assumed to match the founding literature exactly.

### 14.3 US-guided core needle biopsy (CNB)

Core needle biopsy under ultrasound guidance typically uses an 18–20 gauge
automated or semi-automated device, with a posterior-inferior approach from the
superficial parotid tail favored to keep distance from the facial nerve, a needle angle
to the skin kept shallow to avoid the parotid duct and nerve, and real-time color
Doppler used to trace intraparotid vessels and the duct during needle path planning.
Reported complications are described as minimal overall (predominantly hematoma, with
occasional transient facial weakness attributed to local anesthetic infiltration rather
than nerve transection); no confirmed reports of permanent facial nerve injury or tumor
seeding specifically from ultrasound-guided CNB were identified in the literature
reviewed, though systematic, prospective complication-surveillance data specific to this
organ are limited, and needle-gauge-stratified outcome comparisons were not identified.

### 14.4 FNA versus core needle biopsy

This is one of the more consistently evidenced comparative questions in the entire
interventional-ultrasound literature reviewed here. An early direct comparison (Eom et
al., *AJNR* 2015) reported a substantially higher inconclusive rate for FNA than for CNB
(19% versus 4%) and higher tissue-specific diagnostic accuracy for CNB, particularly for
malignant tumors (67% for FNA versus 80% for CNB). A subsequent systematic review and
meta-analysis (Cho et al., *Head Neck* 2020) and, most recently, a large meta-analysis
of 15 studies and 3,669 patients (Kassem et al., *Eur J Radiol* 2025/2026) each concur in
the same direction of effect: **CNB shows consistently higher sensitivity than FNA for
malignancy detection** — reported at approximately 99% for CNB versus approximately 68%
for FNA in the most recent, largest meta-analysis — **with lower non-diagnostic and
repeat-procedure rates, at broadly comparable, low complication rates.** This
directional consistency across three independent studies spanning a decade is among the
strongest comparative-accuracy findings in this entire review, and supports considering
CNB earlier in the diagnostic pathway than reflexive first-line FNA in centers where the
expertise and equipment to perform it safely are available — while recognizing that FNA
remains faster, less invasive, and more widely available, and that the Milan System
provides a mature, standardized reporting framework specifically built around cytology
that CNB-based histopathologic reporting does not yet have an equivalently codified
international equivalent for in this literature.

### 14.5 Rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE)

**No dedicated, well-specified peer-reviewed study of ROSE specifically for
ultrasound-guided salivary gland FNA was identified** with a confirmable citation in
this review's search. This is a notable, explicit gap: ROSE is plausible and commonly
discussed as an adjunct that could reduce non-diagnostic rates, drawing by analogy on
its established role in other organ systems (e.g., pancreatic EUS-FNA), but
salivary-gland-specific comparative outcome data for ROSE were not located and should
not be assumed to transfer directly from other organs.

### 14.6 Role of Color Doppler and CEUS in guiding biopsy

Color Doppler roadmapping to identify and avoid intraparotid vessels during needle
placement is standard, sound interventional practice, consistent with general
image-guided biopsy principles. **No salivary-gland-specific study directly
demonstrating CEUS-guided biopsy target selection** (i.e., using contrast enhancement to
identify viable, non-necrotic tissue for sampling, an approach validated in other organs
such as the mediastinum) **was identified.** Statements to this effect appearing in
adjacent literature generalize from other organ systems and should not be presented as
salivary-gland-specific evidence in the manuscript or in clinical protocols without
explicit qualification.

### 14.7 US-guided drainage and cyst treatment

Ultrasound-guided needle aspiration is a documented, safe alternative to surgical
incision and drainage for parotid abscess, albeit with a higher average number of
repeat procedures required than surgical drainage in at least one comparative study
(Section 9.4). Literature on drainage procedures identified in this review is
concentrated on the parotid gland and the acute suppurative parotitis indication;
submandibular-gland-specific abscess drainage and simple post-traumatic or postoperative
sialocele management literature were not separately identified with confirmable
citations.

### 14.8 Minimally invasive thermal and chemical ablation

Evidence maturity varies substantially by modality and should be stated explicitly
rather than grouped together as a single "emerging techniques" category.

**Radiofrequency ablation (RFA)** is the most evidenced ablation modality identified for
this organ, supported by a prospective safety-and-feasibility trial for Warthin tumor
(reporting approximately 68% volume reduction at a median 45 weeks, with transient
facial or great auricular nerve sensory/motor effects in a minority of patients and no
significant difference from a historical parotidectomy cohort in facial nerve
complication rates), a systematic review of the RFA literature for Warthin tumor, and a
more recent retrospective cohort study of RFA for superficial parotid pleomorphic
adenoma. All identified indications are benign, parotid-gland tumors; RFA for malignant
salivary gland disease was not identified in this review.

**Microwave ablation (MWA)** evidence is limited to small retrospective case series
(a multicenter series of 13 patients with predominantly benign tumors, reporting complete
absorption over a period of months without residual tumor or serious complication, and a
two-center series combining MWA and RFA reporting progressive volume reduction over 12
months) and isolated case reports.

**Ethanol sclerotherapy** evidence is similarly limited — small case series/case reports
for Warthin tumor and parotid cysts, plus at least one registered but not-yet-reported
clinical trial (NCT05221736) — and should be described as an emerging, not established,
technique.

**Laser ablation and high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU)** were, on an extensive
search for this review, **not identified in the salivary-gland literature at all** — not
even as case reports. This is an important distinction from RFA/MWA/ethanol
sclerotherapy: these two modalities should be described as essentially unstudied for
this organ, rather than as "experimental" in the sense of having an early but real case-
level evidence base, since no such evidence base was found to exist.

**Salivary gland ablation for sialorrhea** (image-guided sclerosant injection for
neurologic hypersalivation, typically in pediatric patients) is a distinct clinical
indication from oncologic tumor ablation, using a different technique (dual-drug
sclerosant injection rather than thermal energy) and should not be conflated with tumor-
directed ablation in the manuscript.

### 14.9 Summary appraisal

CNB has **consistent, multiply-reproduced evidence of superiority over FNA for
malignancy detection**, while the Milan System provides a mature but
**institutionally heterogeneous** cytology reporting framework. Among ablation
techniques, only **RFA for benign parotid tumors has a meaningful, if still limited,
prospective evidence base**; MWA and ethanol sclerotherapy are supported by small
case series only; and laser ablation and HIFU are, for this organ, essentially
unstudied rather than merely early-stage.
## 15. Differential diagnosis by sonographic pattern

This section organizes the disease-specific findings of Sections 9–13 around the
sonographic patterns a clinician actually encounters at the point of examination,
rather than around a pre-known diagnosis. Consistent with the evidence reviewed
throughout this manuscript, these are probabilistic, overlapping differentials, not
deterministic sign-to-diagnosis mappings, and are presented as such.

**Diffuse gland enlargement, homogeneous.** Early or mild acute sialadenitis; early
Sjögren's syndrome (before parenchymal inhomogeneity develops); diabetes/obesity-related
sialosis; normal anatomical variation, particularly in younger patients where relative
gland hypoechogenicity is more common (Section 2.6).

**Diffuse gland enlargement, heterogeneous/inhomogeneous.** Chronic or recurrent
sialadenitis; Sjögren's syndrome (OMERACT grade 2–3); IgG4-related sialadenitis, usually
with a more nodular hypoechoic pattern and frequently bilateral lacrimal involvement;
sarcoidosis; diffuse lymphomatous or, less commonly, metastatic infiltration; post-
radiotherapy fibrosis (typically with a supportive history and, where available, elevated
shear-wave stiffness). Doppler, elastography, and OMERACT-style scoring, together with
serology and clinical context, are needed to narrow this differential — grayscale
pattern alone is not sufficient (Sections 9–10).

**Focal hypoechoic lesion, well-defined.** Pleomorphic adenoma; Warthin tumor;
oncocytoma; basal cell adenoma; a benign intraparotid or periparotid lymph node.
Well-defined margins reduce but do not exclude malignancy, given the documented overlap
described in Section 3.

**Focal hypoechoic lesion, ill-defined/infiltrative.** Malignant tumor of any histotype;
less commonly, an aggressive-appearing benign process or an inflammatory focus/abscess
in an appropriate clinical context. This pattern should more strongly prompt tissue
sampling than a well-defined lesion, though the reverse implication — that a
well-defined lesion can be safely observed without sampling — does not follow from the
evidence in Section 3.

**Hypervascular lesion.** Warthin tumor (typically central/mixed pattern on microvascular
imaging); an inflammatory or infected focus; some malignant tumors, though vascularity
alone does not reliably separate benign from malignant given the discordant Doppler
literature reviewed in Section 4.

**Hypovascular/avascular lesion.** Pleomorphic adenoma (typically peripheral or sparse
vascularity); a simple cyst; some malignant tumors presenting at an early stage, per at
least one series documenting poorly vascularized early malignant lesions — an important
caution against treating hypovascularity as reassuring for benignity without other
supporting features (Section 4.3).

**Cystic lesion.** Warthin tumor (partly cystic in a substantial minority of cases);
simple/benign salivary cyst; benign lymphoepithelial cyst (particularly in the context
of HIV infection); a necrotic malignant tumor, especially in the presence of an
irregular, thickened wall or mural nodularity, which should be distinguished from the
thin, regular walls typical of benign cystic change.

**Calcified lesion.** Sialolith, when intraductal with posterior shadowing and
associated ductal dilation, the best-evidenced diagnostic pattern in this entire review
(Section 9.2); coarse calcification within a focal mass, an infrequent but non-specific
finding across both benign and malignant tumor categories.

**Stiff lesion on elastography.** Pleomorphic adenoma (owing to its myxochondroid
stroma) as much as, or more than, some malignant tumors — a pattern for which this
review explicitly cautions against equating stiffness with malignancy (Section 6.3).

**Lesion with marked microvascularity on SMI/MVI.** Warthin tumor most consistently
among benign entities; inflammatory/infective foci; malignant tumors are not
consistently distinguishable from Warthin tumor by microvascular pattern alone on
current evidence (Section 5).

**Lesion with rapid CEUS wash-in/wash-out.** Malignant tumors more often show a
"fast-in, fast-out" pattern with blurred enhancement margins, though pleomorphic
adenoma can mimic this appearance on grayscale/Doppler assessment and CEUS overlap is
similarly documented (Section 7.2).

**Multifocal or bilateral disease.** Warthin tumor (bilateral in a meaningful minority
of cases); Sjögren's syndrome and IgG4-related disease (typically bilateral, symmetric);
sarcoidosis; lymphoma; HIV-related lymphoepithelial disease; metastatic disease to
bilateral intraparotid nodes is comparatively uncommon and should prompt consideration
of the more typical bilateral entities first.

This pattern-based approach is intended as a structured starting point for the
differential diagnosis, to be refined by the technique-specific evidence detailed in
Sections 3–7 and by the disease-specific evidence in Sections 9–13, and ultimately by
tissue sampling where clinical concern warrants it (Section 14).
## 16. Diagnostic workflow

**This workflow is the authors' structured clinical proposal, built from the
technique-by-technique evidence summarized in Sections 3–14. It is explicitly not a
validated algorithm, consensus statement, or society guideline** — no study identified
in this review formally tested a pre-specified combination of all mpUS domains against
a reference standard, as stated in Section 8. It is offered to make the evidence
usable at the point of care while remaining transparent about its evidentiary status.

**Step 1 — Clinical question.** Establish whether the presentation is a diffuse
process (swelling, sicca symptoms, systemic disease) or a focal lesion, and whether
malignancy risk factors are present (rapid growth, pain, facial nerve dysfunction,
fixation, prior radiotherapy, immunosuppression).

**Step 2 — B-mode.** Characterize gland size, echogenicity, and homogeneity, or lesion
morphology (margin, shape, echotexture, cystic change, calcification), and assess
ductal calibre. This step alone is sufficient to complete the evaluation for a
substantial share of presentations — e.g., a classic sialolith with proximal ductal
dilation and a supportive clinical picture (Section 9.2) — without requiring escalation
to advanced technique.

**Step 3 — Color/power Doppler.** Assess for hyperemia (favoring active inflammation)
versus reduced/absent flow (favoring chronic or fibrotic change), and, for a focal
lesion, gross vascularity distribution. Recognize the specificity-over-sensitivity
limitation for malignancy established in Sections 3–4: a non-hypervascular lesion is not
thereby confirmed benign.

**Step 4 — Microvascular imaging, where available.** For a focal lesion where
pleomorphic adenoma versus Warthin tumor is the leading differential, SMI/MVI vascular
pattern analysis adds genuine incremental information (Section 5) and should be used
before proceeding to more resource-intensive techniques when available.

**Step 5 — Elastography.** Useful as a contributory parameter for benign tumor
subtyping and, in the context of suspected Sjögren's syndrome, alongside OMERACT
grey-scale scoring (Sections 6, 10). **Should not be used, alone, as grounds to defer
tissue sampling of a clinically concerning lesion**, given the pleomorphic-adenoma
stiffness confound documented in Section 6.3.

**Step 6 — CEUS, in selected cases.** Reserve for cases where pleomorphic
adenoma-versus-Warthin tumor discrimination remains uncertain after Steps 2–5, or where
perfusion characterization is otherwise likely to change management, recognizing that
CEUS's malignancy-detection evidence is less mature than its benign-tumor-subtyping
evidence (Section 7). Not a substitute for tissue sampling when malignancy is a genuine
concern.

**Step 7 — Tissue sampling (FNA or CNB).** Indicated for any lesion where clinical or
multiparametric findings raise sufficient concern that management would change with a
tissue diagnosis, and should not be deferred indefinitely in pursuit of a non-invasive
diagnosis, given the sensitivity ceiling documented across every imaging technique
reviewed in this manuscript (Section 12.2). Where available and where malignancy is a
genuine clinical concern, this review's evidence synthesis (Section 14.4) supports
considering CNB preferentially over FNA, given its consistently higher reported
sensitivity for malignancy — while acknowledging FNA's advantages in speed, invasiveness,
and the maturity of the Milan System reporting framework built around it.

**Step 8 — CT/MRI, when indicated regardless of ultrasound findings.** MRI (or, for
staging, CT/PET-CT) is required, independent of how reassuring the ultrasound
examination is, whenever: deep parotid lobe or parapharyngeal extension is suspected;
perineural spread is a clinical concern (a feature ultrasound cannot assess, Section
12.3); malignancy has been confirmed or is strongly suspected and staging is needed; or
ultrasound findings are discordant with a strong clinical suspicion of malignancy.

**Step 9 — Surgery or follow-up**, guided by the combined clinical, imaging, and
cytologic/histologic picture developed in Steps 1–8.

**When ultrasound (at any level of sophistication) is not enough.** Deep-lobe/
parapharyngeal disease; suspected perineural spread; nodal and distant staging of
confirmed malignancy; discordance between a reassuring scan and strong clinical
suspicion; and any situation where a confident non-invasive diagnosis cannot be reached
after Steps 2–6 — in each of these situations, escalation to MRI, CT, or tissue
sampling is required, and multiparametric ultrasound should not be used as a reason to
delay that escalation.
## 17. Limitations and unmet needs

**Operator and interobserver factors.** Every technique reviewed in this manuscript —
grayscale interpretation, Doppler settings, strain elastography compression technique,
SWE ROI placement, CEUS timing and bolus technique, and needle-guidance skill for
tissue sampling — is operator-dependent to a degree not fully captured by pooled
diagnostic-accuracy figures derived largely from single, often high-volume, academic
centers. Reproducibility data specific to this organ are strongest for the OMERACT
grey-scale Sjögren's score (Section 10.1), where the training-dependence of reliability
is explicitly documented, and weakest or entirely absent for CEUS and microvascular
imaging, where no dedicated inter-observer or inter-vendor reproducibility study was
identified in this review.

**Lack of standardization.** No universally accepted normal size range exists for any
major salivary gland (Section 2.6); no dedicated society position statement standardizes
Doppler acquisition parameters for this organ (Section 4.1); elastography cut-offs vary
by vendor, ROI, and technique without a harmonized reference range for normal
parenchyma (Section 6); and quantitative CEUS relies on specific third-party perfusion-
analysis software with untested cross-platform comparability for this organ (Section
7.5).

**Vendor and platform dependence.** The microvascular-imaging literature reviewed here
is drawn almost entirely from a single proprietary technology (SMI); no cross-vendor
comparative study for this organ was identified, so it is not established whether
findings generalize to other manufacturers' slow-flow Doppler implementations. Shear-
wave elastography values are well documented elsewhere in the ultrasound literature to
vary by vendor and acquisition method, and the salivary-gland-specific evidence
reviewed here (Section 6.3) is consistent with, though does not independently establish
the mechanism for, this general principle.

**Small sample sizes and retrospective, single-center designs.** With the exception of
the sialolithiasis, FNA-versus-CNB, and Sjögren's-syndrome elastography meta-analyses,
the great majority of studies underlying this review are single-center, retrospective,
and modestly sized (commonly fewer than 100–150 patients), a limitation that applies
with particular force to the microvascular-imaging, CEUS-malignancy-detection, and
ablation-technique literature.

**Selection, spectrum, and reference-standard bias.** Diagnostic-accuracy studies in
this field are predominantly drawn from patients already referred for tissue sampling
or surgery, enriching the study population for clinically significant disease relative
to a general screening population, and reference standards vary across studies (surgical
histopathology, cytology, sialendoscopy, or clinical/serological composite criteria for
autoimmune disease) in ways that are not always fully comparable — a source of
heterogeneity distinct from, but compounding, the technique-heterogeneity discussed
throughout Sections 4–7.

**Histologic heterogeneity and rarity of malignant tumors.** Malignant salivary gland
tumors comprise numerous distinct histotypes, individually uncommon, which limits the
statistical power of any single-center study to characterize histotype-specific
sonographic features — the likely explanation for the near-total absence of dedicated
sonomorphology literature for individual malignant histotypes documented in Section
12.1, rather than a simple failure to study the question.

**Absence of multicenter, prospective validation.** With few exceptions (the
sialolithiasis meta-analysis, the FNA-versus-CNB meta-analyses, the Sjögren's-syndrome
elastography meta-analyses), this review identified essentially no multicenter,
prospectively validated diagnostic-accuracy study for any advanced mpUS technique
addressed here — a limitation that should be weighed heavily by readers considering
adoption of any specific numeric cut-off presented in the primary literature discussed
in Sections 5–7.

**Absence of universally accepted cut-offs.** As detailed at length in Sections 4 and 6,
neither spectral Doppler nor elastography currently supports a single, vendor-
independent numeric threshold for benign-versus-malignant tumor discrimination — a
conclusion this review reaches not merely from absence of consensus but from documented,
mechanistically explained biological and technical heterogeneity that a larger sample
size alone would not resolve.

**Risk of overfitting in proposed multiparametric models.** Because no study identified
here formally validated a pre-specified, algorithmic combination of all mpUS domains
(Section 8), any multiparametric scoring model reported in the primary literature to
date should be regarded as carrying meaningful risk of overfitting to its derivation
cohort until independently, prospectively validated in a separate population — a caution
that applies equally to the emerging AI/radiomics models discussed in Section 18.

**A note on this review's own evidentiary limitations.** As stated in Section 0 of the
accompanying evidence map and repeated here for transparency: the literature synthesis
underlying this manuscript was compiled without the ability to directly open and read
primary-source full texts or abstracts in the environment used to prepare it network
access to PubMed, Crossref, and publisher sites was unavailable throughout preparation.
Every reference cited here was cross-checked across multiple independent search results
for bibliographic consistency, but individual numeric findings should be independently
re-verified by the submitting authors against the original publications before this
manuscript is finalized for submission.
## 18. Future directions

This section is deliberately organized to separate what has already been studied, even
if only preliminarily, from what remains genuinely speculative — a distinction this
review's sources make possible to draw with reasonable confidence.

**Already studied, early-stage.** Ultrasound-based radiomics and deep-learning
classification of salivary gland tumors (benign versus malignant) is a real, active
research area with a modest but growing literature (roughly half a dozen usable
studies, 2022–2025), predominantly single-center, retrospective, and image-level rather
than consistently patient-level validated, reporting accuracy commonly in the 85–95%
range — already the subject of at least one dedicated systematic review and
meta-analysis of AI classification performance (Chau et al., *Otolaryngol Head Neck
Surg* 2026). Automatic segmentation of salivary gland tumors on ultrasound has been
studied, but almost exclusively as a component of a combined segmentation-plus-
diagnosis pipeline in a small number of studies rather than as an independently
validated, multi-architecture-benchmarked task in its own right — a narrower evidence
base than segmentation research in more heavily studied organs. A limited number of
these studies are explicitly multicenter in design, though as retrospective, pooled-
training studies rather than prospective, externally locked-model validation; **no
study identified in this review reports prospective, independent external validation of
a salivary gland ultrasound AI model**, which should be regarded as a prerequisite
before any such model is considered ready for clinical deployment.

**Proposed but not yet formally studied.** Algorithmic, multiparametric data-fusion
models — machine-learning approaches that combine quantitative features from B-mode,
Doppler, elastography, and CEUS into a single fused classifier, as opposed to a
clinician qualitatively synthesizing the same information — were **not identified as
having been formally studied for the salivary glands** in this review's literature
search, despite being a natural extension of the single-modality AI work above and of
the clinician-level multiparametric integration described in Section 8. Likewise, true
image-fusion navigation between ultrasound and MRI or CT (co-registration for real-time
fusion-guided imaging or biopsy, as is established practice in prostate and liver
intervention) was **not identified as having been studied for salivary gland lesions**,
despite the technology existing and being validated in other organs. Both should be
described as plausible, technically feasible extensions of existing work, not as
findings supported by any current salivary-gland-specific evidence.

**Prediction of histology and molecular features.** Beyond binary benign-versus-
malignant classification, prediction of specific histologic subtype or of molecular/
genomic features (e.g., translocation status in mucoepidermoid or adenoid cystic
carcinoma) from ultrasound-derived data was not identified as having been studied for
this organ; MRI-based radiomics work addressing histology prediction in malignant
salivary tumors exists as a methodological precedent and proof-of-principle in the
adjacent MRI literature, but has not, on the evidence reviewed here, been replicated
using ultrasound-derived features.

**Treatment response assessment and theranostics.** Elastography and CEUS have
documented, if still limited, evidence for characterizing post-radiotherapy fibrosis
(Section 6.4), a plausible foundation for treatment-response monitoring, but
longitudinal, treatment-response-specific study designs (as opposed to cross-sectional
irradiated-versus-control comparisons) were not identified. Image-guided minimally
invasive treatment of salivary gland tumors is real but early-stage for RFA and
essentially unstudied for most other ablative modalities (Section 14.8); framing this
area as an established "theranostic" pathway would overstate the current evidence, and
this review declines to do so.

**Overall assessment.** The trajectory of this field — more granular vascular imaging,
increasingly quantitative elastography and CEUS, and now early AI-assisted
classification — is coherent and plausible, and mirrors trajectories seen in other
organ systems (thyroid, breast, liver) where multiparametric and AI-assisted ultrasound
have matured further. However, the salivary gland literature specifically remains, on
the evidence gathered for this review, several steps behind those more mature fields:
single-technique studies predominate over validated multiparametric or AI-fusion
models, and prospective multicenter external validation — the step that would move any
of these techniques from "promising" to "clinically ready" — is largely absent. Future
work that specifically addresses this validation gap, rather than adding further
single-center descriptive studies, would do the most to advance clinical practice in
this field.
## 19. Conclusion

Returning to this review's central question — how conventional and advanced
multiparametric ultrasound can be integrated to improve diagnosis, characterization,
tissue sampling, and management of salivary gland disease, and which components of this
approach are sufficiently validated for current practice — the evidence assembled here
supports a differentiated, technique-specific answer rather than a single verdict on
"multiparametric ultrasound" as a whole.

B-mode and Doppler ultrasound remain the validated foundation of salivary gland
imaging, with sialolithiasis diagnosis and OMERACT grey-scale scoring in Sjögren's
syndrome standing out as the two most rigorously evidenced applications in this entire
review. Microvascular imaging, elastography, and CEUS each add genuine, technique-
specific diagnostic value — most convincingly and convergently for distinguishing
pleomorphic adenoma from Warthin tumor — without yet supporting a universal cut-off for
the broader and more clinically urgent task of excluding malignancy, for reasons that
are partly biological (pleomorphic adenoma's variable stiffness) and partly a matter of
unresolved technique and vendor heterogeneity that this review has analyzed explicitly
rather than smoothed over. Core needle biopsy has more consistent evidence of superior
sensitivity than fine-needle aspiration for malignancy detection, a finding with direct,
actionable implications for how sampling decisions are sequenced in practice. Genuinely
algorithmic multiparametric data fusion, AI-based classification beyond single-center
proof-of-concept, and most ablative techniques beyond radiofrequency ablation remain,
honestly stated, research directions rather than validated clinical tools.

The diagnostic workflow proposed in Section 16 is offered as a structured way to apply
this differentiated evidence base at the point of care, explicitly labeled as the
authors' clinical proposal rather than a validated algorithm. Its central, recurring
message — that a reassuring ultrasound examination, however multiparametric,
reduces but does not eliminate the probability of malignancy — should guide how
confidently any salivary gland ultrasound report is worded, and when the threshold for
tissue sampling or cross-sectional imaging should be crossed. Advancing this field
further will depend less on additional single-center descriptive studies and more on
prospective, multicenter validation of the multiparametric and AI-assisted approaches
that current evidence identifies as promising but not yet proven.

## Clinical boxes

**BOX 1 — Technical pearls**
- Begin bilateral examinations on the presumed normal side for direct comparison
  (Johnson et al. 2025).
- Non-visualization of Stensen's or Wharton's duct is the normal finding, not a
  technical failure; sialagogue provocation can transiently aid visualization when
  obstruction is suspected.
- The retromandibular vein is the standard surrogate landmark for the (non-visualized)
  facial nerve plane within the parotid gland.
- Minimize probe pressure during Doppler/microvascular imaging acquisition; even
  moderate compression can occlude low-pressure flow and produce a falsely avascular
  impression.
- No single grayscale, Doppler, elastographic, or CEUS feature is individually
  sufficient for a confident malignant/benign diagnosis — interpret in combination and
  in clinical context.

**BOX 2 — Pitfalls**
- Well-defined margins do not exclude malignancy; a meaningful proportion of malignant
  tumors present with reassuringly well-defined margins on grayscale imaging.
- High stiffness on elastography is not synonymous with malignancy: pleomorphic
  adenoma's myxochondroid stroma can be as stiff as, or stiffer than, some malignant
  tumors.
- Hilar preservation on lymph node ultrasound does not exclude metastatic or
  lymphomatous involvement, particularly in early nodal disease.
- Ultrasound cannot assess perineural spread or reliably characterize deep parotid
  lobe/parapharyngeal disease — do not over-interpret a "clean" superficial scan in a
  clinical scenario where these are concerns.
- Numeric elastography and Doppler cut-offs reported in individual studies are
  frequently vendor- and technique-specific; they should not be applied as universal
  thresholds outside the setting in which they were derived.

**BOX 3 — When to use CEUS**
- Persistent diagnostic uncertainty between pleomorphic adenoma and Warthin tumor after
  B-mode, Doppler, and elastographic assessment, where quantitative time-intensity-curve
  parameters have genuine, pooled-evidence support.
- Selected cases where perfusion characterization is likely to materially change
  management, understanding that CEUS's evidence for malignancy detection specifically
  is less mature and not yet supported by pooled diagnostic-accuracy data.
- Not a substitute for tissue sampling when malignancy is a genuine clinical concern.

**BOX 4 — When to biopsy, and FNA vs core needle biopsy**
- Biopsy any lesion where clinical or multiparametric findings raise sufficient concern
  that management would change with a tissue diagnosis; do not defer sampling
  indefinitely in pursuit of a reassuring non-invasive multiparametric picture.
- Where CNB expertise and equipment are available and malignancy is a genuine concern,
  consider CNB preferentially: independent comparative studies consistently report
  higher sensitivity for malignancy and lower non-diagnostic rates than FNA, at
  comparable complication rates.
- FNA remains reasonable as a faster, less invasive first step, particularly for
  lesions with a low pretest probability of malignancy, and benefits from the maturity
  of the Milan System reporting framework.

**BOX 5 — When ultrasound is not enough**
- Suspected deep parotid lobe or parapharyngeal extension.
- Suspected perineural tumor spread.
- Nodal or distant staging of confirmed or strongly suspected malignancy.
- Discordance between a reassuring ultrasound examination (at any level of
  sophistication) and strong clinical suspicion of malignancy.
- Any situation where a confident, actionable diagnosis has not been reached after
  structured multiparametric assessment — proceed to tissue sampling and/or
  cross-sectional imaging rather than repeating or escalating ultrasound technique
  alone.
