## 6. Elastography

### 6.1 Strain elastography

Strain elastography derives a qualitative or semiquantitative measure of tissue
deformability from manual or physiological (e.g., pulsatile) compression, typically
displayed as a color-coded elasticity map and summarized either as a categorical
elasticity score or as a strain ratio between the lesion and adjacent reference tissue.
Its principal technical limitations are operator dependence in applying a controlled,
reproducible compression force, sensitivity to lesion depth (deeper lesions receive
attenuated and less uniform compression), and dependence on the composition of the
reference tissue chosen for the ratio, none of which are unique to the salivary glands
but all of which apply with particular force in this superficial but anatomically
variable organ.

### 6.2 Shear-wave elastography

Shear-wave elastography (2D-SWE, and point SWE where used) instead measures the
propagation velocity of acoustic radiation-force-induced shear waves through tissue,
reported either as a velocity (m/s) or converted to a Young's modulus-based stiffness
estimate (kPa), and is generally regarded as more operator-independent and quantitative
than strain elastography, though acquisition still requires attention to region-of-
interest (ROI) placement, minimal probe pressure, adequate acoustic window, and, per
general SWE methodology, multiple acquisitions with a reported median or mean value
rather than a single measurement.

### 6.3 Benign versus malignant tumor differentiation: an instructive case of biological and technical heterogeneity

Shear-wave elastography of salivary gland tumors is one of the clearest illustrations in
this entire review of why a single universal cut-off is not achievable, for reasons that
are partly technical and partly biological. In an early, influential series (Bhatia et
al., *Eur Radiol* 2012, n = 60 lesions), median shear modulus was, counter to intuition,
*lower* in malignant lesions (13.5 kPa) than in benign lesions (18.3 kPa) as a group,
because pleomorphic adenoma — the most common benign tumor — can itself be markedly
stiff (median 22.5 kPa, versus 16.9 kPa for Warthin tumor) owing to its myxochondroid
stromal component, while two mucoepidermoid carcinomas in the same series were
substantial stiffness outliers (81.9 and 132.0 kPa). This is not merely inter-study
noise: it is a stated, mechanistically explained biological confound — a benign tumor
subtype can be stiffer than some malignant tumors — meaning that even a perfectly
reproducible, vendor-independent SWE measurement could not, in principle, cleanly
separate benign from malignant salivary tumors using stiffness alone.

Consistent with this, two systematic reviews/meta-analyses addressing ostensibly the
same clinical question — elastography (strain and/or shear-wave, pooled) for benign-
versus-malignant salivary tumor differentiation — report substantially different pooled
estimates: a 2022 meta-analysis of 16 studies (1,105 patients) reported pooled
sensitivity 0.73 and specificity 0.64 (Wu et al., *Front Oncol* 2022), while a 2025
meta-analysis of 26 studies (1,609 patients) reported pooled sensitivity 0.75 and
specificity 0.79 (*BMC Oral Health* 2025) — an approximately 15-percentage-point gap in
pooled specificity for nominally the same question, three years apart, with different
(though overlapping) primary-study sets. The earlier meta-analysis explicitly attributes
between-study heterogeneity chiefly to sonoelastographic measurement technique
(qualitative score versus semiquantitative strain ratio versus quantitative SWE), noting
that quantitative and semiquantitative methods outperformed purely qualitative scoring.
Taken together, the biological confound described above and this magnitude of
inter-meta-analysis discordance mean that **no single kPa or m/s cut-off for benign-
versus-malignant salivary tumor differentiation can currently be recommended**, and any
reported cut-off value in an individual primary study should be understood as
local/vendor/technique-specific rather than generalizable.

### 6.4 Elastography in radiation-induced fibrosis

A smaller but directionally consistent body of evidence supports SWE as a marker of
post-radiotherapy salivary gland fibrosis: parotid and submandibular stiffness have been
reported significantly higher in irradiated glands than in healthy controls in more than
one small case-control study, correlating in at least one series with reduced salivary
flow and xerostomia severity. This is a mechanistically plausible, diffuse-disease
application distinct from focal tumor characterization, and while the evidence base is
still limited to small, largely single-center studies, the direction of effect is
consistent across the studies identified.

### 6.5 Elastography in Sjögren's syndrome

This is the most consistently supported diagnostic elastography application identified
in this review. At least three independent systematic reviews or meta-analyses were
published within an approximately 18-month window (2023–2024) addressing salivary gland
elastography in Sjögren's syndrome or sicca symptoms, of which the most detailed
retrievable pooled estimate reports 15 studies (816 patients, 735 controls) with pooled
sensitivity 0.80 and specificity 0.87, with formal meta-regression identifying country,
diagnostic criteria, vendor/imaging system, specific SWE technique, measurement
location, control-group definition, and patient age as plausible sources of
heterogeneity (*Eur Radiol* 2023). That three separate, near-contemporaneous systematic
reviews addressed the same question is itself informative: it suggests the primary
evidence base, while reasonably consistent in pooled sensitivity/specificity, is still
regarded by investigators as unsettled enough to warrant repeated re-synthesis, and no
single elastography value should be presented as a stand-alone diagnostic threshold for
Sjögren's syndrome independent of the semiquantitative OMERACT grey-scale framework
discussed in Section 10, with which several recent studies have explicitly attempted
combination.

### 6.6 Summary appraisal

Elastography's most defensible current clinical role is as a *contributory*, not
stand-alone, parameter: promising for benign tumor subtyping and reasonably well
supported (pooled sensitivity/specificity in the 0.80s) as an adjunct in Sjögren's
syndrome, but explicitly **not supported by the evidence for a universal cut-off in
benign-versus-malignant tumor differentiation**, for reasons that are partly a genuine
biological limit (pleomorphic adenoma stiffness) and partly a matter of unresolved
technique and vendor heterogeneity. Reviews and clinical protocols that present a single
numeric elastography threshold for malignancy risk without qualifying vendor and
technique should be read with this heterogeneity explicitly in mind.
