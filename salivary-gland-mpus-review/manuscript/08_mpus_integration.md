## 8. Multiparametric ultrasound: an integration framework

Sections 3–7 examined each ultrasound domain in isolation because that is how the
evidence base is structured — the overwhelming majority of studies identified in this
review test one technique, or at most two, against a single reference standard, rather
than testing a pre-specified combination of all five domains as a single index test. It
is therefore important to be explicit about a distinction that is easy to blur: there is
a meaningful difference between *clinicians combining several techniques' output
qualitatively during interpretation* (well represented in the literature, e.g., Wakonig
et al.'s quantitative multiparametric protocols combining B-mode, elastography, and
CEUS) and a *formally validated, pre-specified multiparametric diagnostic algorithm*
with its own prospectively tested accuracy (essentially unrepresented in the literature
identified for this review). Most of what is described as "multiparametric ultrasound"
in the current salivary gland literature is the former, not the latter.

With that distinction stated, the following framework reflects how the techniques in
Sections 3–7 can be layered onto one another in clinical practice, in order of
increasing complexity, cost, and evidentiary maturity:

**B-mode** establishes the presence, location, and gross morphology of a lesion or
diffuse process and generates the initial differential diagnosis (Section 3). **Color/
power Doppler** adds a first, low-cost read on vascularity, most useful for
distinguishing active inflammation from quiescent disease and for a coarse read on
tumor vascularity, albeit with acknowledged limits for benign-versus-malignant
discrimination (Section 4). **Microvascular imaging (SMI/MVI)**, where available, adds
finer-grained vascular architecture information, currently best supported for
pleomorphic adenoma-versus-Warthin tumor subtyping (Section 5). **Elastography** adds
mechanical (stiffness) information that is genuinely informative for benign tumor
subtyping and, particularly, for Sjögren's syndrome, while carrying an important
biological caveat that limits its use for malignancy exclusion (Section 6). **CEUS**
adds perfusion-kinetic information, well supported for the same pleomorphic
adenoma-versus-Warthin tumor question and promising, though less robustly pooled, for
broader malignancy assessment (Section 7). **Tissue sampling** (Section 14) remains the
step that resolves diagnostic uncertainty that imaging — at any level of
sophistication — cannot resolve on its own, and should not be deferred indefinitely in
pursuit of a non-invasive multiparametric diagnosis when clinical concern for
malignancy is significant.

This ordering reflects typical acquisition sequence and increasing technical/resource
burden, not a validated diagnostic algorithm with its own prospectively measured
accuracy: **no study identified in this review formally tested a pre-specified
combination of all five domains against a single reference standard with pooled
diagnostic-accuracy reporting for the combination as a whole.** The closest available
evidence — studies combining, for example, elastography with CEUS, or SWE with
grayscale-based prediction models — do report incremental improvement in area-under-
the-curve performance over single-technique assessment in the specific cohorts studied,
which is a reasonable basis for clinical practice, but should not be over-generalized
into a claim that multiparametric ultrasound as a composite test has been formally
validated. The workflow proposed in Section 16 of this review should accordingly be read
as **the authors' structured clinical proposal, explicitly not a validated algorithm or
society guideline**, built from the technique-by-technique evidence summarized above and
intended to make that evidence usable at the point of care while being transparent about
where it rests on consolidated data and where it rests on extrapolation and clinical
judgment.
