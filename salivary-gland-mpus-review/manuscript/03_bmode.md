## 3. Conventional B-mode ultrasound

Systematic grayscale assessment of a salivary gland or focal lesion should address, in
order: overall gland size and symmetry; parenchymal echogenicity and homogeneity;
presence, number, location, and characteristics of any focal lesion (size, shape,
margin definition, internal echogenicity, presence of cystic change or necrosis,
calcification, posterior acoustic features); ductal calibre; and the relationship of
any abnormality to adjacent muscle, bone, and vessels.

**Diffuse parenchymal change.** Diffuse glandular enlargement with heterogeneous,
coarsened, or hypoechoic parenchyma is a nonspecific pattern shared by acute and chronic
sialadenitis, Sjögren's syndrome, IgG4-related disease, sarcoidosis, and, less commonly,
diffuse lymphomatous or metastatic infiltration; grayscale appearance alone cannot
reliably separate these entities, and the pattern should be considered a trigger for
further clinical, serological, and — where advanced techniques or biopsy are indicated —
multiparametric work-up rather than a diagnosis in itself (see Sections 9–10).

**Focal lesions.** Margin definition, shape, and internal echo pattern are the classical
descriptors used to stratify benign-versus-malignant risk. Well-defined margins,
smoothly lobulated shape, and homogeneous hypoechoic echotexture with posterior acoustic
enhancement are the textbook description of benign lesions such as pleomorphic adenoma,
while irregular or ill-defined margins, heterogeneous echotexture, and infiltrative
growth into adjacent tissue are the textbook description of malignancy. In practice, the
discriminating value of these signs is materially weaker than this dichotomy suggests:
in one histopathologically confirmed series, grayscale-and-Doppler assessment achieved
high specificity but only moderate sensitivity for malignancy (60% sensitivity, 95.2%
specificity, 90.3% accuracy), meaning a substantial minority of malignant tumors were
missed despite careful conventional assessment (Rzepakowska et al., *Eur Arch
Otorhinolaryngol* 2017). Margin definition in particular should not be treated as a
reliably sensitive sign: in at least one reported dataset, well-defined margins were
present in a majority of malignant tumors, not only in benign ones, and irregular shape
was similarly common in both groups — an overlap that must be stated plainly rather than
smoothed into a simple rule. A pooled meta-analysis of grayscale ultrasound (independent
of Doppler) for salivary tumor diagnosis reported similarly Doppler-independent overall
figures around 63% sensitivity and 92% specificity, again illustrating a specificity-
over-sensitivity pattern (Liu et al., *Oral Surg Oral Med Oral Pathol Oral Radiol* 2015).
This pattern — grayscale ultrasound as a better "rule-in" than "rule-out" test for
malignancy — recurs throughout the more advanced techniques discussed later in this
review and is one of its recurring themes.

**Cystic and necrotic change.** Anechoic or predominantly cystic components with thin,
regular walls are common in Warthin tumor (reported in a substantial minority to near-
half of cases in comparative series) and comparatively uncommon in pleomorphic adenoma;
irregular, thick-walled cystic or necrotic change within an otherwise solid mass raises
concern for malignancy, particularly in a rapidly enlarging lesion, but is not by itself
diagnostic of any single entity.

**Calcification and pseudocapsule.** Coarse or punctate calcification within a focal
lesion is an infrequent but recognized feature that should prompt closer scrutiny,
though it is not specific to malignancy. Pleomorphic adenoma is often described as
having a thin, well-defined "pseudocapsule" (a compressed rim of surrounding tissue
rather than a true fibrous capsule), which correlates with its typically well-defined
sonographic margin — a useful concept for understanding why margin sharpness alone is an
imperfect malignancy discriminator, since a benign tumor with true capsular invasion
risk (as in malignant transformation, discussed in Section 12) can still appear
deceptively well-marginated.

**Ductal dilation and calculi.** Duct dilation proximal to an obstructing calculus is a
reliable, well-evidenced grayscale finding, and ultrasound detection of sialoliths
themselves is one of the best-validated diagnostic applications of salivary gland
ultrasound overall (see Section 9.2), typically appearing as an echogenic focus with
posterior acoustic shadowing, though small or intraductal stones without significant
acoustic impedance mismatch can be missed, particularly distally within Wharton's duct
where mandibular shadowing can obscure the field.

**General principle.** No single grayscale feature — margin, shape, echogenicity,
cystic change, or calcification — has been shown to carry the level of specificity
needed to make an unsupported diagnosis of malignancy or of a specific histologic
subtype. Grayscale findings are best used to generate a structured differential
diagnosis and to determine whether the lesion warrants advanced multiparametric
assessment, tissue sampling, or cross-sectional imaging, rather than as a stand-alone
diagnostic endpoint (see Section 15 for pattern-based differential-diagnostic
reasoning).
