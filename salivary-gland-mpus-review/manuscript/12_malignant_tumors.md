## 12. Malignant salivary gland tumors

### 12.1 General limitations of ultrasound for histologic classification

Malignant salivary gland tumors — mucoepidermoid carcinoma, adenoid cystic carcinoma,
acinic cell carcinoma, salivary duct carcinoma, carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma,
metastatic disease, and lymphoma among others — share, on grayscale and Doppler
ultrasound, a broadly overlapping appearance of hypoechogenicity, variable margin
definition, heterogeneous echotexture, and variably increased vascularity, without
reliable sonographic features that predict specific histology. This is a structural
limitation, not a gap that better technique alone will close: ultrasound characterizes
gross tissue architecture and perfusion, not the cytological and molecular features that
define histologic subtype, and this review found **no dedicated, confirmable
primary-literature study establishing histotype-specific grayscale or Doppler
sonographic signatures for mucoepidermoid carcinoma, adenoid cystic carcinoma, acinic
cell carcinoma, salivary duct carcinoma, or carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma**
specifically, as distinct from generic "malignant salivary mass" features. Diagnostic
literature addressing these specific histotypes is predominantly cytology-based (see
Section 14.2, the Milan System), not sonomorphology-based. Carcinoma ex pleomorphic
adenoma is best suspected from clinical history — a long-standing parotid mass with
recent rapid growth, pain, or new facial nerve palsy — rather than from a specific
sonographic sign.

### 12.2 What ultrasound can and cannot tell the clinician about malignancy risk

As established in Section 3, conventional grayscale-and-Doppler assessment is
better at ruling malignancy *in* (high specificity, in the 90–95% range across the
comparative studies identified) than ruling it *out* (moderate sensitivity, commonly in
the 60–66% range), meaning a substantial minority of malignant tumors present with a
reassuringly benign-appearing conventional ultrasound examination. This
specificity-over-sensitivity pattern recurs across the advanced techniques reviewed in
Sections 4–7 as well — elastography and CEUS add diagnostically useful information in
specific comparisons (particularly benign tumor subtyping) but no technique reviewed
here, alone or in the combinations actually tested in the literature, achieves
sensitivity high enough to safely exclude malignancy without tissue sampling in a
clinically concerning lesion. This is a central, recurring message of this review and
should directly inform the workflow proposed in Section 16: **a benign-appearing
multiparametric ultrasound examination reduces, but does not eliminate, the probability
of malignancy**, and the threshold for tissue sampling should be set accordingly rather
than deferred in reliance on a reassuring scan.

### 12.3 Signs of local infiltration, perineural spread, and extraglandular extension

Infiltrative growth into adjacent muscle or loss of the normal tissue-plane interface is
a grayscale feature that, when clearly present, raises substantial concern for
malignancy, though its absence does not exclude malignancy given the overlap described
above. **Ultrasound has no meaningful, direct role in the assessment of perineural
tumor spread** — a well-recognized feature of adenoid cystic carcinoma in particular —
because this requires visualization of nerve fascicles and foraminal/skull-base
anatomy well beyond the depth and field of view accessible to ultrasound; this
assessment is the specific province of MRI, and this limitation should be stated
plainly wherever perineural spread is a clinical concern, rather than left implicit.
Similarly, deep-lobe or parapharyngeal extraglandular extension is frequently
underestimated or entirely missed by ultrasound because of acoustic shadowing from the
mandible, and is again an indication for MRI rather than more advanced ultrasound
technique (see Section 16 for when cross-sectional imaging is required regardless of US
findings).

### 12.4 Metastatic disease and lymphoma

Metastasis to the salivary glands, most commonly the parotid via intraparotid lymph
nodes, arises predominantly from head-and-neck cutaneous primaries (cutaneous squamous
cell carcinoma of the scalp and face being the most frequently cited source), with
sonographic features of the involved node(s) — solid, hypoechoic, ill-defined margins,
a tendency toward central necrosis, occasionally multiple involved nodes — generally
following the same reasoning applied to metastatic cervical lymphadenopathy elsewhere
(Section 13). Salivary gland (typically MALT-type) lymphoma, which can arise as a
complication of longstanding Sjögren's syndrome, has been described with a "linear
echogenic strands" or multiple small hypoechoic nodule pattern in a segmental
distribution, though this review was unable to identify a well-characterized primary
citation specifically substantiating this descriptor for salivary parenchymal (as
opposed to nodal) lymphoma, and this should be regarded as a provisional descriptive
pattern pending better primary-source support.

### 12.5 Summary appraisal

Ultrasound, at any level of technical sophistication reviewed in this manuscript, is
**better at raising suspicion of malignancy than at excluding it, and has essentially no
established role in predicting specific malignant histotype or in assessing perineural
spread or deep extraglandular extension**. These are structural, not merely
evidence-immature, limitations, and should shape how confidently any ultrasound report
— conventional or multiparametric — is worded when malignancy is in the differential.
