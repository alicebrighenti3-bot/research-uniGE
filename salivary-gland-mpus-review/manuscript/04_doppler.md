## 4. Color Doppler and power Doppler

### 4.1 Technique and optimization

Color and power Doppler assessment of salivary gland lesions requires attention to
pulse repetition frequency (PRF) and wall filter settings low enough to detect the
relatively slow, low-volume flow typical of small intraglandular vessels, without
introducing excessive motion/flash artifact from patient or probe movement; gain should
be set just below the level of background noise. Box size should be limited to the
region of interest to preserve frame rate and color sensitivity, and probe pressure
should be minimized, since even moderate compression can occlude low-pressure venous
and small arterial flow and produce a falsely avascular appearance. No dedicated,
salivary-gland-specific technical-optimization literature addressing these parameters
was identified in this review's search; the recommendations above extrapolate from
general superficial-organ Doppler principles rather than from a salivary-gland-specific
validation study, and this should be understood as a technique gap, not a settled
protocol.

### 4.2 Normal and inflammatory vascular patterns

The normal parenchyma of the major salivary glands shows sparse, symmetric flow on
color/power Doppler. Acute sialadenitis characteristically produces diffuse, increased
parenchymal flow (hyperemia), a pattern that is sensitive for active inflammation but
not specific to its cause (bacterial, viral, or autoimmune). Chronic sialadenitis and
glandular atrophy tend to show reduced or absent flow. This qualitative, largely
pattern-recognition use of Doppler — present versus absent, increased versus reduced,
symmetric versus asymmetric — is well established descriptively but has not, in the
literature identified here, been reduced to a validated quantitative scoring system
outside the Sjögren's-syndrome-specific OMERACT framework discussed in Section 10.

### 4.3 Doppler in focal lesion characterization: what the evidence actually supports

Spectral Doppler parameters — resistive index (RI), pulsatility index (PI), and peak
systolic velocity (PSV) — have been proposed as quantitative discriminators between
benign and malignant salivary gland tumors since at least the classic single-center
study by Bradley et al. (*Clin Radiol* 2000), which, in a cohort of 56 patients, reported
that RI and PI values above certain thresholds were associated with malignancy with
reasonable reported sensitivity and high specificity. This remains the most frequently
cited spectral-Doppler cut-off study in this field, but it is a single, relatively
small, single-center series, and **no completed systematic review or meta-analysis
pooling RI/PI/PSV cut-offs for salivary gland tumor characterization was identified** —
a 2021-era protocol registered for exactly this purpose does not appear, from this
review's search, to have been published as a completed review. Other, smaller series
report discordant, sometimes contradictory conclusions: one study reported no significant
difference in vascularity grade between a majority of benign and a majority of malignant
tumors, with most malignant cases in that series presenting at an early, poorly
vascularized stage — a finding directly at odds with a simple "malignant = hypervascular"
heuristic. Combined color-Doppler-plus-MRI approaches have shown incremental value over
either modality alone for lesion localization and characterization in some series, but
detailed combined-accuracy figures were not consistently retrievable for this review.

**The practical implication is that spectral Doppler indices should not be presented, on
current evidence, as a validated numeric cut-off for benign-versus-malignant
discrimination.** Qualitative Doppler pattern (peripheral versus central/mixed
vascularity, presence of penetrating vessels) is a more consistently reported and more
directly comparable feature across studies (and is discussed further alongside
microvascular imaging in Section 5, since many of the studies reporting these patterns
use SMI or comparable slow-flow techniques rather than conventional color Doppler
alone).

### 4.4 Doppler in lymph node evaluation

In cervical lymph nodes generally (not restricted to the salivary-gland nodal basins
specifically), preserved hilar or "parallel" vascular branching is characteristic of
reactive nodes, while peripheral, subcapsular, or otherwise aberrant vascular
distribution raises concern for metastatic or lymphomatous involvement — a pattern
better characterized by microvascular imaging than by conventional Doppler in the
comparative literature available (see Section 5.4 and Section 13).

### 4.5 Summary appraisal

Qualitative color/power Doppler assessment (presence/absence and gross distribution of
flow) is a well-established, low-cost adjunct to grayscale imaging with genuine clinical
utility, particularly for inflammatory disease. Quantitative spectral Doppler cut-offs
for tumor characterization remain **promising but unvalidated**: supported by a small
number of studies with discordant findings and no confirmed pooled meta-analytic
estimate, and should not be presented to patients or in clinical reports as
diagnostically definitive.
