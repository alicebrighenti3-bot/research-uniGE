## 15. Differential diagnosis by sonographic pattern

This section organizes the disease-specific findings of Sections 9–13 around the
sonographic patterns a clinician actually encounters at the point of examination,
rather than around a pre-known diagnosis. Consistent with the evidence reviewed
throughout this manuscript, these are probabilistic, overlapping differentials, not
deterministic sign-to-diagnosis mappings, and are presented as such.

**Diffuse gland enlargement, homogeneous.** Early or mild acute sialadenitis; early
Sjögren's syndrome (before parenchymal inhomogeneity develops); diabetes/obesity-related
sialosis; normal anatomical variation, particularly in younger patients where relative
gland hypoechogenicity is more common (Section 2.6).

**Diffuse gland enlargement, heterogeneous/inhomogeneous.** Chronic or recurrent
sialadenitis; Sjögren's syndrome (OMERACT grade 2–3); IgG4-related sialadenitis, usually
with a more nodular hypoechoic pattern and frequently bilateral lacrimal involvement;
sarcoidosis; diffuse lymphomatous or, less commonly, metastatic infiltration; post-
radiotherapy fibrosis (typically with a supportive history and, where available, elevated
shear-wave stiffness). Doppler, elastography, and OMERACT-style scoring, together with
serology and clinical context, are needed to narrow this differential — grayscale
pattern alone is not sufficient (Sections 9–10).

**Focal hypoechoic lesion, well-defined.** Pleomorphic adenoma; Warthin tumor;
oncocytoma; basal cell adenoma; a benign intraparotid or periparotid lymph node.
Well-defined margins reduce but do not exclude malignancy, given the documented overlap
described in Section 3.

**Focal hypoechoic lesion, ill-defined/infiltrative.** Malignant tumor of any histotype;
less commonly, an aggressive-appearing benign process or an inflammatory focus/abscess
in an appropriate clinical context. This pattern should more strongly prompt tissue
sampling than a well-defined lesion, though the reverse implication — that a
well-defined lesion can be safely observed without sampling — does not follow from the
evidence in Section 3.

**Hypervascular lesion.** Warthin tumor (typically central/mixed pattern on microvascular
imaging); an inflammatory or infected focus; some malignant tumors, though vascularity
alone does not reliably separate benign from malignant given the discordant Doppler
literature reviewed in Section 4.

**Hypovascular/avascular lesion.** Pleomorphic adenoma (typically peripheral or sparse
vascularity); a simple cyst; some malignant tumors presenting at an early stage, per at
least one series documenting poorly vascularized early malignant lesions — an important
caution against treating hypovascularity as reassuring for benignity without other
supporting features (Section 4.3).

**Cystic lesion.** Warthin tumor (partly cystic in a substantial minority of cases);
simple/benign salivary cyst; benign lymphoepithelial cyst (particularly in the context
of HIV infection); a necrotic malignant tumor, especially in the presence of an
irregular, thickened wall or mural nodularity, which should be distinguished from the
thin, regular walls typical of benign cystic change.

**Calcified lesion.** Sialolith, when intraductal with posterior shadowing and
associated ductal dilation, the best-evidenced diagnostic pattern in this entire review
(Section 9.2); coarse calcification within a focal mass, an infrequent but non-specific
finding across both benign and malignant tumor categories.

**Stiff lesion on elastography.** Pleomorphic adenoma (owing to its myxochondroid
stroma) as much as, or more than, some malignant tumors — a pattern for which this
review explicitly cautions against equating stiffness with malignancy (Section 6.3).

**Lesion with marked microvascularity on SMI/MVI.** Warthin tumor most consistently
among benign entities; inflammatory/infective foci; malignant tumors are not
consistently distinguishable from Warthin tumor by microvascular pattern alone on
current evidence (Section 5).

**Lesion with rapid CEUS wash-in/wash-out.** Malignant tumors more often show a
"fast-in, fast-out" pattern with blurred enhancement margins, though pleomorphic
adenoma can mimic this appearance on grayscale/Doppler assessment and CEUS overlap is
similarly documented (Section 7.2).

**Multifocal or bilateral disease.** Warthin tumor (bilateral in a meaningful minority
of cases); Sjögren's syndrome and IgG4-related disease (typically bilateral, symmetric);
sarcoidosis; lymphoma; HIV-related lymphoepithelial disease; metastatic disease to
bilateral intraparotid nodes is comparatively uncommon and should prompt consideration
of the more typical bilateral entities first.

This pattern-based approach is intended as a structured starting point for the
differential diagnosis, to be refined by the technique-specific evidence detailed in
Sections 3–7 and by the disease-specific evidence in Sections 9–13, and ultimately by
tissue sampling where clinical concern warrants it (Section 14).
