## Clinical boxes

**BOX 1 — Technical pearls**
- Begin bilateral examinations on the presumed normal side for direct comparison
  (Johnson et al. 2025).
- Non-visualization of Stensen's or Wharton's duct is the normal finding, not a
  technical failure; sialagogue provocation can transiently aid visualization when
  obstruction is suspected.
- The retromandibular vein is the standard surrogate landmark for the (non-visualized)
  facial nerve plane within the parotid gland.
- Minimize probe pressure during Doppler/microvascular imaging acquisition; even
  moderate compression can occlude low-pressure flow and produce a falsely avascular
  impression.
- No single grayscale, Doppler, elastographic, or CEUS feature is individually
  sufficient for a confident malignant/benign diagnosis — interpret in combination and
  in clinical context.

**BOX 2 — Pitfalls**
- Well-defined margins do not exclude malignancy; a meaningful proportion of malignant
  tumors present with reassuringly well-defined margins on grayscale imaging.
- High stiffness on elastography is not synonymous with malignancy: pleomorphic
  adenoma's myxochondroid stroma can be as stiff as, or stiffer than, some malignant
  tumors.
- Hilar preservation on lymph node ultrasound does not exclude metastatic or
  lymphomatous involvement, particularly in early nodal disease.
- Ultrasound cannot assess perineural spread or reliably characterize deep parotid
  lobe/parapharyngeal disease — do not over-interpret a "clean" superficial scan in a
  clinical scenario where these are concerns.
- Numeric elastography and Doppler cut-offs reported in individual studies are
  frequently vendor- and technique-specific; they should not be applied as universal
  thresholds outside the setting in which they were derived.

**BOX 3 — When to use CEUS**
- Persistent diagnostic uncertainty between pleomorphic adenoma and Warthin tumor after
  B-mode, Doppler, and elastographic assessment, where quantitative time-intensity-curve
  parameters have genuine, pooled-evidence support.
- Selected cases where perfusion characterization is likely to materially change
  management, understanding that CEUS's evidence for malignancy detection specifically
  is less mature and not yet supported by pooled diagnostic-accuracy data.
- Not a substitute for tissue sampling when malignancy is a genuine clinical concern.

**BOX 4 — When to biopsy, and FNA vs core needle biopsy**
- Biopsy any lesion where clinical or multiparametric findings raise sufficient concern
  that management would change with a tissue diagnosis; do not defer sampling
  indefinitely in pursuit of a reassuring non-invasive multiparametric picture.
- Where CNB expertise and equipment are available and malignancy is a genuine concern,
  consider CNB preferentially: independent comparative studies consistently report
  higher sensitivity for malignancy and lower non-diagnostic rates than FNA, at
  comparable complication rates.
- FNA remains reasonable as a faster, less invasive first step, particularly for
  lesions with a low pretest probability of malignancy, and benefits from the maturity
  of the Milan System reporting framework.

**BOX 5 — When ultrasound is not enough**
- Suspected deep parotid lobe or parapharyngeal extension.
- Suspected perineural tumor spread.
- Nodal or distant staging of confirmed or strongly suspected malignancy.
- Discordance between a reassuring ultrasound examination (at any level of
  sophistication) and strong clinical suspicion of malignancy.
- Any situation where a confident, actionable diagnosis has not been reached after
  structured multiparametric assessment — proceed to tissue sampling and/or
  cross-sectional imaging rather than repeating or escalating ultrasound technique
  alone.
