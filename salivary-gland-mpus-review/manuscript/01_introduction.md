## 1. Introduction

Ultrasound is the first-line imaging modality for the major salivary glands. The
parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands are superficial structures accessible to
high-frequency linear-array transducers, which afford spatial resolution that neither
computed tomography (CT) nor magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can match for the
glandular parenchyma itself and for the immediately adjacent duct system. Ultrasound
adds real-time assessment of vascularity, dynamic evaluation with compression and
sialagogue provocation, and the possibility of image-guided sampling in the same
sitting as diagnostic imaging — capabilities that cross-sectional imaging does not
offer at the point of care. Its limitations are equally well established: a narrow
field of view that cannot reliably assess the deep parotid lobe, the parapharyngeal
space, or perineural extension along the facial or lingual nerves; marked operator
dependence; and no direct role in nodal or distant staging of malignant disease, where
CT and MRI (and, for staging, PET/CT) remain indispensable (Bialek et al., *RadioGraphics*
2006; Gritzmann et al., *Eur Radiol* 2003).

Over the past two decades, ultrasound practice in this organ system has moved from a
purely grayscale, morphology-based examination toward what is increasingly described as
*multiparametric ultrasound* (mpUS): the structured, combined use of B-mode imaging,
color and power Doppler, microvascular imaging techniques capable of depicting slow
flow (e.g., Superb Microvascular Imaging, SMI), strain and shear-wave elastography, and
contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), interpreted together rather than in isolation
(Martino et al., *Gland Surg* 2020). For the purposes of this review, **mpUS is defined
as the deliberate, protocol-driven acquisition and joint interpretation of two or more
of these ultrasound domains — morphologic, vascular, mechanical (stiffness), and
perfusion-based — applied to the same lesion or gland**, as distinct from the
incidental or non-systematic use of a single ancillary technique.

This evolution matters clinically because the differential diagnosis of salivary gland
disease spans mechanistically distinct categories — obstructive/inflammatory (sialolithiasis,
sialadenitis), autoimmune/systemic (Sjögren's syndrome, IgG4-related disease), benign
neoplastic, and malignant neoplastic — that can present with overlapping clinical
findings (glandular swelling, pain, a palpable mass) but require different management
pathways. Grayscale ultrasound alone has documented, non-trivial error rates in this
discrimination, particularly for malignancy, where sensitivity is frequently lower than
specificity even in well-conducted single-center series (Rzepakowska et al., *Eur Arch
Otorhinolaryngol* 2017). The rationale for mpUS is that vascular, mechanical, and
perfusion information, layered onto morphology, can narrow this diagnostic uncertainty
and better select which lesions require tissue sampling, which can be safely observed,
and which require cross-sectional imaging before any decision is made.

Whether this rationale is fully realized in the current evidence base is a separate
question from whether it is plausible, and is the central question this review is
organized around: **how can conventional and advanced multiparametric ultrasound be
integrated to improve the diagnosis, characterization, tissue sampling, and management
of normal and pathological salivary glands, and which components of this approach are
sufficiently validated for current clinical practice?** As will be shown throughout this
review, the honest answer varies by technique and by clinical question: some
applications (sialolithiasis diagnosis, OMERACT grey-scale scoring in Sjögren's
syndrome, the superiority of core needle biopsy over fine-needle aspiration for
malignancy detection) rest on consistent, multiply-reproduced evidence; others
(elastographic and Doppler cut-offs for benign-versus-malignant differentiation,
algorithmic multiparametric data fusion) remain promising but unvalidated, or frankly
experimental. This review does not attempt to catalogue every published sonographic
sign of every salivary gland disease; rather, it aims to show, technique by technique
and disease by disease, where mpUS measurably changes clinical reasoning and where it
does not yet.

Several existing publications cover overlapping ground and are explicitly acknowledged
here rather than left for the reader to discover independently. Martino et al.
(*Gland Surgery*, 2020) published a narrative review under an almost identical
conceptual framing, restricted to the parotid gland and predating essentially all of
the microvascular-imaging, most of the CEUS-quantification, and all of the
artificial-intelligence literature discussed below. A 2025 continuing-medical-education
review by Johnson et al. (*Ultraschall in der Medizin*) covers sonography of salivary
gland tumors and disorders in the same target-journal category as the present work; its
precise technique structure was not independently verifiable during preparation of this
manuscript and should be considered a closely related, contemporaneous resource rather
than a document this review claims to supersede. The present review differs from both
in scope (all major glands and the full disease spectrum, including lymph nodes, in a
single technique-by-technique structure), in its explicit grading of evidence strength
and analysis of discordance between studies rather than descriptive cataloguing, in its
integration of interventional ultrasound into a single diagnostic pathway, and in its
currency through 2026.
