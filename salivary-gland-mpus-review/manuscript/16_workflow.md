## 16. Diagnostic workflow

**This workflow is the authors' structured clinical proposal, built from the
technique-by-technique evidence summarized in Sections 3–14. It is explicitly not a
validated algorithm, consensus statement, or society guideline** — no study identified
in this review formally tested a pre-specified combination of all mpUS domains against
a reference standard, as stated in Section 8. It is offered to make the evidence
usable at the point of care while remaining transparent about its evidentiary status.

**Step 1 — Clinical question.** Establish whether the presentation is a diffuse
process (swelling, sicca symptoms, systemic disease) or a focal lesion, and whether
malignancy risk factors are present (rapid growth, pain, facial nerve dysfunction,
fixation, prior radiotherapy, immunosuppression).

**Step 2 — B-mode.** Characterize gland size, echogenicity, and homogeneity, or lesion
morphology (margin, shape, echotexture, cystic change, calcification), and assess
ductal calibre. This step alone is sufficient to complete the evaluation for a
substantial share of presentations — e.g., a classic sialolith with proximal ductal
dilation and a supportive clinical picture (Section 9.2) — without requiring escalation
to advanced technique.

**Step 3 — Color/power Doppler.** Assess for hyperemia (favoring active inflammation)
versus reduced/absent flow (favoring chronic or fibrotic change), and, for a focal
lesion, gross vascularity distribution. Recognize the specificity-over-sensitivity
limitation for malignancy established in Sections 3–4: a non-hypervascular lesion is not
thereby confirmed benign.

**Step 4 — Microvascular imaging, where available.** For a focal lesion where
pleomorphic adenoma versus Warthin tumor is the leading differential, SMI/MVI vascular
pattern analysis adds genuine incremental information (Section 5) and should be used
before proceeding to more resource-intensive techniques when available.

**Step 5 — Elastography.** Useful as a contributory parameter for benign tumor
subtyping and, in the context of suspected Sjögren's syndrome, alongside OMERACT
grey-scale scoring (Sections 6, 10). **Should not be used, alone, as grounds to defer
tissue sampling of a clinically concerning lesion**, given the pleomorphic-adenoma
stiffness confound documented in Section 6.3.

**Step 6 — CEUS, in selected cases.** Reserve for cases where pleomorphic
adenoma-versus-Warthin tumor discrimination remains uncertain after Steps 2–5, or where
perfusion characterization is otherwise likely to change management, recognizing that
CEUS's malignancy-detection evidence is less mature than its benign-tumor-subtyping
evidence (Section 7). Not a substitute for tissue sampling when malignancy is a genuine
concern.

**Step 7 — Tissue sampling (FNA or CNB).** Indicated for any lesion where clinical or
multiparametric findings raise sufficient concern that management would change with a
tissue diagnosis, and should not be deferred indefinitely in pursuit of a non-invasive
diagnosis, given the sensitivity ceiling documented across every imaging technique
reviewed in this manuscript (Section 12.2). Where available and where malignancy is a
genuine clinical concern, this review's evidence synthesis (Section 14.4) supports
considering CNB preferentially over FNA, given its consistently higher reported
sensitivity for malignancy — while acknowledging FNA's advantages in speed, invasiveness,
and the maturity of the Milan System reporting framework built around it.

**Step 8 — CT/MRI, when indicated regardless of ultrasound findings.** MRI (or, for
staging, CT/PET-CT) is required, independent of how reassuring the ultrasound
examination is, whenever: deep parotid lobe or parapharyngeal extension is suspected;
perineural spread is a clinical concern (a feature ultrasound cannot assess, Section
12.3); malignancy has been confirmed or is strongly suspected and staging is needed; or
ultrasound findings are discordant with a strong clinical suspicion of malignancy.

**Step 9 — Surgery or follow-up**, guided by the combined clinical, imaging, and
cytologic/histologic picture developed in Steps 1–8.

**When ultrasound (at any level of sophistication) is not enough.** Deep-lobe/
parapharyngeal disease; suspected perineural spread; nodal and distant staging of
confirmed malignancy; discordance between a reassuring scan and strong clinical
suspicion; and any situation where a confident non-invasive diagnosis cannot be reached
after Steps 2–6 — in each of these situations, escalation to MRI, CT, or tissue
sampling is required, and multiparametric ultrasound should not be used as a reason to
delay that escalation.
