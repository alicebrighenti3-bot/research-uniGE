## 14. Interventional ultrasound

### 14.1 US-guided fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC)

Ultrasound-guided FNA is a well-established, low-morbidity first-line sampling technique
for salivary gland lesions, with reported sensitivity and specificity that vary widely
across single-institution series (roughly 60–100% sensitivity and 90–100% specificity
across the range of studies identified in this review), reflecting differences in
operator experience, aspirate adequacy, and case mix rather than a single stable
performance figure. **No large-scale, multi-institutional meta-analysis specifically
quantifying pooled sensitivity/specificity of ultrasound-guided (as opposed to blind or
palpation-guided) FNA for salivary gland lesions** was identified with confirmed pooled
numbers; the wide reported range above should be understood as a synthesis across
heterogeneous single-institution series, not a validated pooled estimate.

### 14.2 The Milan System for Reporting Salivary Gland Cytopathology

The Milan System for Reporting Salivary Gland Cytopathology (MSRSGC), first published in
2018 under the sponsorship of the American Society of Cytopathology and the
International Academy of Cytology, provides a standardized six-tiered diagnostic
category framework (non-diagnostic; non-neoplastic; atypia of undetermined
significance, AUS; benign neoplasm; salivary gland neoplasm of uncertain malignant
potential, SUMP; suspicious for malignancy; malignant), each associated with an
estimated implied risk of malignancy intended to guide management. A second edition,
co-published simultaneously across three cytopathology journals in 2023/2024, refined
risk-of-malignancy estimates using accumulated systematic-review/meta-analysis evidence,
added imaging guidance, and updated ancillary/molecular testing recommendations while
retaining the original six-tier structure. **No further update beyond this 2023/2024
second edition was identified as published through 2026.**

Institutional validation studies applying the Milan System report **substantial
inter-institutional heterogeneity in observed risk-of-malignancy figures**, particularly
for the lower-volume, diagnostically ambiguous categories — AUS and SUMP — where
reported risk of malignancy has ranged, across different single-center series identified
in this review, from roughly one-half to values considerably higher or lower depending
on institution and case mix. This heterogeneity is a genuine, acknowledged limitation of
the system as currently implemented, not merely a reporting artifact, and should be
represented as such: the Milan System standardizes terminology and reporting structure
effectively, but the specific risk-of-malignancy percentage attached to each category by
any individual institution should be interpreted with its own local validation data in
mind rather than assumed to match the founding literature exactly.

### 14.3 US-guided core needle biopsy (CNB)

Core needle biopsy under ultrasound guidance typically uses an 18–20 gauge
automated or semi-automated device, with a posterior-inferior approach from the
superficial parotid tail favored to keep distance from the facial nerve, a needle angle
to the skin kept shallow to avoid the parotid duct and nerve, and real-time color
Doppler used to trace intraparotid vessels and the duct during needle path planning.
Reported complications are described as minimal overall (predominantly hematoma, with
occasional transient facial weakness attributed to local anesthetic infiltration rather
than nerve transection); no confirmed reports of permanent facial nerve injury or tumor
seeding specifically from ultrasound-guided CNB were identified in the literature
reviewed, though systematic, prospective complication-surveillance data specific to this
organ are limited, and needle-gauge-stratified outcome comparisons were not identified.

### 14.4 FNA versus core needle biopsy

This is one of the more consistently evidenced comparative questions in the entire
interventional-ultrasound literature reviewed here. An early direct comparison (Eom et
al., *AJNR* 2015) reported a substantially higher inconclusive rate for FNA than for CNB
(19% versus 4%) and higher tissue-specific diagnostic accuracy for CNB, particularly for
malignant tumors (67% for FNA versus 80% for CNB). A subsequent systematic review and
meta-analysis (Cho et al., *Head Neck* 2020) and, most recently, a large meta-analysis
of 15 studies and 3,669 patients (Kassem et al., *Eur J Radiol* 2025/2026) each concur in
the same direction of effect: **CNB shows consistently higher sensitivity than FNA for
malignancy detection** — reported at approximately 99% for CNB versus approximately 68%
for FNA in the most recent, largest meta-analysis — **with lower non-diagnostic and
repeat-procedure rates, at broadly comparable, low complication rates.** This
directional consistency across three independent studies spanning a decade is among the
strongest comparative-accuracy findings in this entire review, and supports considering
CNB earlier in the diagnostic pathway than reflexive first-line FNA in centers where the
expertise and equipment to perform it safely are available — while recognizing that FNA
remains faster, less invasive, and more widely available, and that the Milan System
provides a mature, standardized reporting framework specifically built around cytology
that CNB-based histopathologic reporting does not yet have an equivalently codified
international equivalent for in this literature.

### 14.5 Rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE)

**No dedicated, well-specified peer-reviewed study of ROSE specifically for
ultrasound-guided salivary gland FNA was identified** with a confirmable citation in
this review's search. This is a notable, explicit gap: ROSE is plausible and commonly
discussed as an adjunct that could reduce non-diagnostic rates, drawing by analogy on
its established role in other organ systems (e.g., pancreatic EUS-FNA), but
salivary-gland-specific comparative outcome data for ROSE were not located and should
not be assumed to transfer directly from other organs.

### 14.6 Role of Color Doppler and CEUS in guiding biopsy

Color Doppler roadmapping to identify and avoid intraparotid vessels during needle
placement is standard, sound interventional practice, consistent with general
image-guided biopsy principles. **No salivary-gland-specific study directly
demonstrating CEUS-guided biopsy target selection** (i.e., using contrast enhancement to
identify viable, non-necrotic tissue for sampling, an approach validated in other organs
such as the mediastinum) **was identified.** Statements to this effect appearing in
adjacent literature generalize from other organ systems and should not be presented as
salivary-gland-specific evidence in the manuscript or in clinical protocols without
explicit qualification.

### 14.7 US-guided drainage and cyst treatment

Ultrasound-guided needle aspiration is a documented, safe alternative to surgical
incision and drainage for parotid abscess, albeit with a higher average number of
repeat procedures required than surgical drainage in at least one comparative study
(Section 9.4). Literature on drainage procedures identified in this review is
concentrated on the parotid gland and the acute suppurative parotitis indication;
submandibular-gland-specific abscess drainage and simple post-traumatic or postoperative
sialocele management literature were not separately identified with confirmable
citations.

### 14.8 Minimally invasive thermal and chemical ablation

Evidence maturity varies substantially by modality and should be stated explicitly
rather than grouped together as a single "emerging techniques" category.

**Radiofrequency ablation (RFA)** is the most evidenced ablation modality identified for
this organ, supported by a prospective safety-and-feasibility trial for Warthin tumor
(reporting approximately 68% volume reduction at a median 45 weeks, with transient
facial or great auricular nerve sensory/motor effects in a minority of patients and no
significant difference from a historical parotidectomy cohort in facial nerve
complication rates), a systematic review of the RFA literature for Warthin tumor, and a
more recent retrospective cohort study of RFA for superficial parotid pleomorphic
adenoma. All identified indications are benign, parotid-gland tumors; RFA for malignant
salivary gland disease was not identified in this review.

**Microwave ablation (MWA)** evidence is limited to small retrospective case series
(a multicenter series of 13 patients with predominantly benign tumors, reporting complete
absorption over a period of months without residual tumor or serious complication, and a
two-center series combining MWA and RFA reporting progressive volume reduction over 12
months) and isolated case reports.

**Ethanol sclerotherapy** evidence is similarly limited — small case series/case reports
for Warthin tumor and parotid cysts, plus at least one registered but not-yet-reported
clinical trial (NCT05221736) — and should be described as an emerging, not established,
technique.

**Laser ablation and high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU)** were, on an extensive
search for this review, **not identified in the salivary-gland literature at all** — not
even as case reports. This is an important distinction from RFA/MWA/ethanol
sclerotherapy: these two modalities should be described as essentially unstudied for
this organ, rather than as "experimental" in the sense of having an early but real case-
level evidence base, since no such evidence base was found to exist.

**Salivary gland ablation for sialorrhea** (image-guided sclerosant injection for
neurologic hypersalivation, typically in pediatric patients) is a distinct clinical
indication from oncologic tumor ablation, using a different technique (dual-drug
sclerosant injection rather than thermal energy) and should not be conflated with tumor-
directed ablation in the manuscript.

### 14.9 Summary appraisal

CNB has **consistent, multiply-reproduced evidence of superiority over FNA for
malignancy detection**, while the Milan System provides a mature but
**institutionally heterogeneous** cytology reporting framework. Among ablation
techniques, only **RFA for benign parotid tumors has a meaningful, if still limited,
prospective evidence base**; MWA and ethanol sclerotherapy are supported by small
case series only; and laser ablation and HIFU are, for this organ, essentially
unstudied rather than merely early-stage.
