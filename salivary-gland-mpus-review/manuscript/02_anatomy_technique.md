## 2. Normal anatomy and ultrasound technique

### 2.1 Parotid gland

The parotid is the largest major salivary gland, situated in the preauricular and
retromandibular region, bounded superficially by skin and subcutaneous fat, deeply by
the styloid process and its associated musculature, anteriorly by the masseter muscle,
and posteriorly by the external ear and sternocleidomastoid muscle. On ultrasound it
appears as a homogeneously hyperechoic gland relative to adjacent muscle, owing to its
fat content, with a fine, uniform echotexture. Ultrasound cannot directly visualize the
facial nerve, which is not an ultrasound landmark itself but is localized indirectly:
the retromandibular vein, which runs superficial and lateral to the nerve trunk within
the gland, is the standard surrogate landmark separating the notional superficial and
deep lobes for reporting purposes (Bialek et al., *RadioGraphics* 2006). An
ultrasonographic technique using Stensen's duct as an alternative localizing criterion
for the facial nerve plane relative to intraparotid tumors has also been described,
though it is less widely used than the retromandibular-vein landmark. The deep parotid
lobe and any parapharyngeal extension of disease are not reliably assessed by
ultrasound because of acoustic shadowing from the mandibular ramus; this is one of the
central, unavoidable limitations of the technique and a principal reason MRI remains
necessary for deep-lobe or parapharyngeal disease.

Stensen's duct (the parotid duct) courses anteriorly over the masseter, turns medially
at the anterior masseteric border, and pierces the buccinator to open opposite the
second upper molar. On normal ultrasound the duct is **not visualized unless dilated**;
non-visualization is itself the expected normal finding, not an indication of technical
failure. Transient ductal distension can be provoked with a sialagogue (e.g., ascorbic
acid or lemon juice) to aid visualization when duct patency or a subtle stricture is in
question, though this maneuver is more a diagnostic adjunct for suspected obstruction
than part of the routine baseline examination.

An accessory parotid gland — a discrete lobule of parotid tissue anterior to the main
gland, superficial to the masseter and related to the buccal branches of the facial
nerve and the transverse facial artery — is a recognized and clinically relevant
anatomical variant, because tumors can arise within it and be overlooked if it is not
specifically sought with a high-frequency transducer. Reported prevalence figures for
the accessory parotid gland vary substantially between series (from roughly one-fifth to
over two-thirds of subjects, depending on whether cadaveric, CT, or clinical-imaging
methodology is used), reflecting differences in detection method rather than true
biological variability, and no single figure should be presented as definitive.

### 2.2 Submandibular gland

The submandibular gland lies in the submandibular triangle, bounded by the anterior and
posterior bellies of the digastric muscle and the inferior border of the mandible, and
wraps around the posterior free edge of the mylohyoid muscle to give rise to a smaller
deep extension. It is typically slightly less echogenic and coarser in texture than the
parotid gland but remains hyperechoic relative to adjacent muscle in health. The facial
artery and vein course along its posterior and lateral surface at the mandibular angle
and are consistent vascular landmarks on grayscale and color Doppler imaging.

Wharton's duct (the submandibular duct) runs anteromedially along the floor of the
mouth, deep to the mylohyoid and superficial to the hyoglossus and genioglossus
muscles, to open at the sublingual caruncle. As with Stensen's duct, the normal duct is
not visualized on ultrasound unless dilated. A formal, society-endorsed proposal for
standardized submandibular gland ultrasound terminology and technique was published by
the Salivary Gland Committee of the American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck
Surgery (Hoffman et al., *Laryngoscope Investig Otolaryngol* 2024); at the time of this
review's preparation, a corresponding standardization document for the parotid gland
had not been identified as published, and none for the sublingual or minor glands — an
explicit gap that should be closed by future methodological work.

### 2.3 Sublingual gland and minor salivary glands

The sublingual gland, the smallest of the three major paired glands, lies in the floor
of the mouth between the mylohyoid muscle inferolaterally and the genioglossus muscle
medially, superior to the mylohyoid sling. It drains via numerous small excretory ducts
(commonly cited as roughly 8–20) that open independently along the sublingual fold or,
variably, join Wharton's duct. Its small size and deep, midline floor-of-mouth location
make complete sonographic depiction inherently more operator- and patient-dependent
than for the parotid or submandibular glands.

Minor salivary glands, distributed throughout the oral mucosa, lips, and palate, are
only inconsistently accessible to transcutaneous ultrasound; when assessed
sonographically, this is most often in the specific research/clinical context of
labial minor gland evaluation as an alternative or complement to biopsy in suspected
Sjögren's syndrome, where a minimum number of visualized glands and a minimum
cross-sectional area threshold have been used as feasibility criteria in at least one
comparative study against histopathology. This should be regarded as a specialized,
disease-context application rather than part of the routine normal-anatomy survey.

### 2.4 Lymph nodes and vascular anatomy

Intraparotid and periparotid lymph nodes are a normal anatomical finding, most
consistently encountered near the parotid tail and along the course of the
retromandibular vein; small, oval nodes with a preserved echogenic fatty hilum are
expected and should not be over-interpreted. Periglandular cervical nodal groups
(submandibular, upper jugular chain) are routinely included in a complete examination
given their relevance to both inflammatory and malignant disease of the glands they
drain. Relevant regional vascular anatomy includes the external carotid artery and its
branches (facial, superficial temporal, transverse facial, maxillary arteries) and the
retromandibular and facial veins, all of which serve both as anatomical landmarks and as
structures that must be identified and avoided during image-guided intervention (see
Section 14).

### 2.5 Examination technique

A high-frequency linear-array transducer is used for all three major glands; reported
frequency ranges vary slightly across sources (commonly cited ranges span approximately
5–14 MHz), without full consensus on a single standard, and this variability should
itself be acknowledged rather than presented as settled. A lower-frequency curved-array
transducer (approximately 5–7 MHz) is a useful adjunct for reaching the deep parotid
lobe in selected patients, accepting the trade-off in resolution. The patient is
typically examined supine with the neck extended and rotated away from the side under
examination; both transverse and longitudinal scan planes are obtained for each gland.
Bilateral, side-to-side comparison is explicitly recommended in recent guidance, with
the examination conventionally beginning on the presumed normal side (Johnson et al.,
*Ultraschall Med* 2025). Gain, depth, and focal-zone position should be optimized for
the superficial location of these glands, and tissue harmonic imaging can be used to
improve contrast resolution and reduce artifact, particularly in patients with a larger
body habitus, though dedicated salivary-gland-specific comparative data on harmonic
versus fundamental imaging were not identified in this review's literature search.
Probe-compression technique, while a general principle of superficial-structure
ultrasound (useful, for instance, in distinguishing a compressible vascular structure
or in displacing overlying bowel gas elsewhere in the body), has not been specifically
studied for the salivary glands in the literature identified here and is not addressed
by dedicated protocols.

### 2.6 Normal size, echogenicity, and the absence of universal reference ranges

Normal gland dimensions have been reported since at least the late 1990s from
small, single-center biometric studies, but **no universally accepted, cross-vendor
normal size range for the parotid or submandibular gland exists**, a limitation stated
explicitly by the most recent standardization effort in this field (Hoffman et al.,
*Laryngoscope Investig Otolaryngol* 2024). Echogenicity is similarly not a fixed,
binary normal/abnormal parameter: in the largest modern cohort identified addressing
this question (n = 969 adults with sonographically normal glands), the submandibular
gland was homogeneously hypoechoic relative to the parotid in roughly a quarter of
subjects overall, and the proportion of subjects showing this relative hypoechogenicity
fell substantially with increasing age — from roughly three-fifths of the youngest age
group to just over one-fifth of those aged 50 and older (Choi et al., *Ultrasonography*
2021). Age is therefore a documented, quantifiable confound on what counts as "normal"
echogenicity, and body mass index and metabolic status (diabetes, obesity, sialosis)
have also been reported to influence glandular echogenicity, homogeneity, and border
definition, independent of any change in tissue stiffness on elastography. Practically,
this means normal-gland assessment should rely on side-to-side symmetry and on
qualitative pattern recognition calibrated to the patient's age and body habitus, rather
than on a fixed numeric size or echogenicity threshold.

Duct calibre is a further area where available reference data are frequently
misapplied: normative diameter figures for Stensen's and Wharton's ducts in the
literature derive from sialography (contrast fluoroscopy) or cadaveric morphometry, not
from ultrasound itself, and no ultrasound-specific normative duct-diameter dataset was
identified. Because the normal duct is not visualized on ultrasound, the clinically
meaningful "normal" finding is non-visualization rather than a measured calibre — a
distinction that should be made explicit whenever duct size is discussed in a
sonographic (as opposed to sialographic) context.
